25  Homework 7: Bayesian Linear Regression

Your numbers will not match mine, and that is fine

Every result in this course depends on a random seed, and often also on your library versions, your hardware, and how many CPU threads your machine uses. Two people running identical, correct code will get different numbers — sometimes noticeably different.

What is graded is that you used the right model and the right procedure, and that your interpretation follows from the numbers you actually obtained. Quote your own output.

What is not graded is matching any particular value. Do not tune your way toward a number you saw on a slide or heard from a classmate — that is the one way to turn this into a wrong answer.

One caveat worth knowing. The broad ordering of models usually survives this variation. If yours comes out backwards from what the lecture found, say so in a sentence and offer a reason: either you have found something real about your run, or there is a bug worth hunting. Both are worth more marks than quietly reporting a number you do not believe.

25.1 Overview

Bayesian linear regression replaces OLS point estimates with full posterior distributions over every coefficient. Instead of asking “Is beta significant at p < 0.05?”, you ask “What is the probability that this effect is positive?” and “Is the effect large enough to matter for business decisions?”

This final homework fits a Bayesian regression model for store sales as a function of promotions, competition, and store characteristics. It also introduces Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) as a tool for deciding which variables to include and how to interpret the coefficients causally.

Learning objectives:

  • Draw and interpret a DAG for a business forecasting model
  • Specify weakly informative priors for regression coefficients
  • Read posterior coefficient distributions (HDI, probability of direction)
  • Apply ROPE analysis to distinguish statistical from practical significance
  • Use posterior samples to simulate conditional scenarios

25.2 Dataset and Setup

The question for this assignment is a causal one: how much does demand respond to price? That is an elasticity, and it is the number a category manager would actually act on.

import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import pymc as pm
import arviz as az
import statsmodels.api as sm
import warnings, logging
warnings.filterwarnings('ignore')
logging.getLogger('pymc').setLevel(logging.ERROR)

from pathlib import Path

def data_path(name):
    for base in (Path.cwd(), *Path.cwd().parents):
        p = base / "data" / "processed" / name
        if p.exists():
            return p
    raise FileNotFoundError(
        f"Could not find data/processed/{name}. "
        "Run `python scripts/prep_m5.py` from the repository root first."
    )

RANDOM_STATE = 42

weekly = (pd.read_csv(data_path("m5_weekly.csv"), parse_dates=["week_start"])
            .sort_values(["store_id", "cat_id", "week_start"]))
weekly["series"] = weekly.store_id + "_" + weekly.cat_id

df = weekly.dropna(subset=["units", "avg_price"]).copy()
df["ly"] = np.log(df.units.clip(lower=1))          # log units
df["lp"] = np.log(df.avg_price)                     # log price
df["t"]  = (df.week_start - df.week_start.min()).dt.days / 365.25
df["snap"]  = (df.snap_days >= 5).astype(float)     # high-SNAP week
df["event"] = (df.event_days > 0).astype(float)     # any event that week
codes, uniq = pd.factorize(df.series)
df["idx"] = codes
J = len(uniq)

# Centre log price within series. NOT standardized -- see the note below.
df["lpc"] = df.lp - df.groupby("series").lp.transform("mean")

print(f"{len(df):,} rows | {J} series | "
      f"price range {df.avg_price.min():.2f}-{df.avg_price.max():.2f}")

Why logs on both sides. With ly and lp both logged, the price coefficient is an elasticity: the percentage change in units for a one percent change in price. That is directly interpretable, and it is what makes the scenario analysis in Q6 possible.

Do not standardize log price

It is a common reflex, and here it is a trap. Dividing lp by its standard deviation rescales the coefficient into “per standard deviation of log price” — which is no longer an elasticity, and every percentage claim you build on it in Q6 will be wrong by the factor you divided by. Leave lp in log units. Every predictor in this assignment is already on a scale a manager reads directly: log price in percent, snap and event as 0/1, t in years.

Centring within series (lpc) is different and is fine — it shifts the variable without rescaling it, leaves the elasticity untouched, and greatly improves sampling, because price levels differ across series and otherwise correlate with the intercepts.

25.4.3 Q3 — Bayesian Linear Regression (Suggested prompts: 3)

Fit the controlled specification from Q2(b) as a Bayesian model — price, SNAP, events, trend, and a per-series intercept. Use weakly informative priors:

  • beta_j ~ Normal(0, 1) for each predictor (weakly informative on these scales)
  • alpha ~ Normal(0, 1) for the intercept
  • sigma ~ HalfNormal(1) for observation noise

Give the per-series intercepts a hierarchical prior, written non-centred, as in Homework 6.

Sample with pm.sample(1000, tune=1500, chains=4, target_accept=0.9) and report the posterior mean, sd and 94% HDI for each coefficient — plus \(\hat{R}\), ESS and divergences.

Ask ArviZ for the HDI explicitly

az.summary(idata) reports an 89% equal-tailed interval by default, not the HDI. For the highest-density interval this course uses, call az.summary(idata, ci_kind="hdi", ci_prob=0.94), or az.hdi(samples, prob=0.94) on raw draws.

An equal-tailed interval cuts 5.5% off each tail; an HDI is the shortest interval holding 94% of the mass. They coincide for a symmetric posterior and diverge for a skewed one.

# Build and sample Bayesian linear regression

Your response: Compare the posterior means to the controlled OLS coefficients from Q2(b). With this much data they should agree closely — say whether they do, and explain why agreement is the expected result rather than a disappointing one.

Then: what does a wide HDI tell you that a narrow one does not, and which coefficient here has the widest relative to its mean?

25.4.5 Q5 — ROPE Analysis (Suggested prompts: 2)

“Credible” and “big enough to act on” are different questions. A ROPE — region of practical equivalence — makes the second one explicit.

For each predictor, compute the fraction of posterior mass inside |beta| < 0.05. Because nothing here is standardized, that threshold reads directly: for price, an elasticity between \(-0.05\) and \(0.05\) means a 1% price change moves units by less than 0.05% — no one would act on it. For snap and event it means a weekly lift under 5%.

Present results in a table:

Predictor Posterior Mean 94% HDI % in ROPE Practically significant?

A predictor is “practically significant” if less than 5% of its posterior falls in the ROPE.

# Compute % of posterior in ROPE for each predictor

Your response: Name any predictor that is credible but negligible — HDI excluding zero while most of its posterior sits inside the ROPE. That combination is common in large samples and is exactly what a p-value cannot express.

What should a manager do with such a finding, and what would have to change for it to become worth acting on?
