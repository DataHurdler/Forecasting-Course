1  Lab 1: Benchmarks and Exponential Smoothing

Run this one live: the executable lab.

1.1 What we are doing

Lecture 1 made a claim that sounded like housekeeping: always beat a benchmark first. In the next 35 minutes you are going to test that claim on a real Walmart series, and then watch it fail on a different one.

The business question: you are forecasting weekly HOUSEHOLD-goods demand at store TX_1 to set inventory a year out. Which method should you actually ship?

Suggested time: ~35 minutes · You will need: pandas, numpy, matplotlib, statsmodels, and data/processed/m5_weekly.csv (run python scripts/prep_m5.py if it is missing).

1.2 Setup

Nothing to fill in here — run it and move on.

import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from statsmodels.tsa.holtwinters import ExponentialSmoothing, SimpleExpSmoothing, Holt
import warnings
warnings.filterwarnings('ignore')

from pathlib import Path

def data_path(name):
    """Find the processed data file whether you render from the repo root or from
    this file's own folder."""
    for base in (Path.cwd(), *Path.cwd().parents):
        p = base / "data" / "processed" / name
        if p.exists():
            return p
    raise FileNotFoundError(
        f"Could not find data/processed/{name}. "
        "Run `python scripts/prep_m5.py` from the repository root first."
    )

weekly = pd.read_csv(data_path("m5_weekly.csv"), parse_dates=["week_start"])

def get_series(store, cat):
    return (weekly[(weekly.store_id == store) & (weekly.cat_id == cat)]
            .set_index("week_start")["units"].sort_index().astype(float))

y = get_series("TX_1", "HOUSEHOLD")

N_TEST = 52
train, test = y.iloc[:-N_TEST], y.iloc[-N_TEST:]
print(f"{len(y)} weeks total — train {len(train)}, test {len(test)}")

Notice: the series roughly doubles over five years, and the level is still moving at the end. Hold that thought — it is the whole lab.

Walkthrough

Never fit anything before you’ve looked at the series. This takes ten seconds and it will save you an hour.

Two things. First, the level roughly doubles across five years — this store’s household aisle is growing, and growing substantially. Second, and this is the one to hold onto: the level is still moving at the end of the training window. It hasn’t flattened out. Whatever we fit has to say something about where the level goes next.

That single observation is the whole lab. Everything that happens in the next twenty minutes is a consequence of the level moving.

1.4 Step 2 — Establish the benchmark

Before any model, the thing to beat. Seasonal naive says: this week next year looks like this week last year.

def rmse(actual, pred):
    return float(np.sqrt(np.mean((np.asarray(actual) - np.asarray(pred)) ** 2)))

def mae(actual, pred):
    return float(np.mean(np.abs(np.asarray(actual) - np.asarray(pred))))

seasonal_naive = y.shift(52).iloc[-N_TEST:].values

results = {"Seasonal naive": (rmse(test, seasonal_naive), mae(test, seasonal_naive))}
print(f"Seasonal naive  RMSE {results['Seasonal naive'][0]:,.0f}")

1.5 Step 3 — Fit the three models

All three calls come straight from Lecture 1. Run this, then predict the ordering before you look at Step 4 — write your guess down.

# SES — level only.
ses_fit = SimpleExpSmoothing(train).fit()
results["SES"] = (rmse(test, ses_fit.forecast(N_TEST)),
                  mae(test, ses_fit.forecast(N_TEST)))

# Holt — level + trend. Undamped for now; we come back to damping in Step 5.
holt_fit = Holt(train).fit()
results["Holt"] = (rmse(test, holt_fit.forecast(N_TEST)),
                   mae(test, holt_fit.forecast(N_TEST)))

# Holt-Winters — level + trend + seasonality, additive, period 52.
hw_fit = ExponentialSmoothing(train, trend="add",
                              seasonal="add", seasonal_periods=52).fit()
results["Holt-Winters"] = (rmse(test, hw_fit.forecast(N_TEST)),
                           mae(test, hw_fit.forecast(N_TEST)))
One constraint to respect

Holt-Winters must estimate a seasonal index for each of the 52 weeks, and it needs two full cycles — 104 observations — to initialize. You have 225 training weeks, so you are fine here. You will not always be.

Walkthrough

(Screen: run the three-model cell. It takes a few seconds — Holt-Winters is estimating 52 seasonal indices.)

Three models, three lines, all straight from Lecture 1. SES — level only. No trend, no seasonality. The simplest thing that could work.

Holt — level plus trend. Undamped for now; we come back to that in Step 5, and the fact that I’m flagging it should tell you something. Holt-Winters — level, trend, and seasonality, additive, period 52.

One constraint worth respecting. Holt-Winters estimates a seasonal index for every one of the 52 weeks, and it needs two full cycles — 104 observations — just to initialize. You have 225 training weeks, so you’re fine. You will not always be fine. When you get to a series with three years of weekly data and you’re wondering why Holt-Winters won’t fit, this is why.

Look at where Holt’s forecast goes.

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(11, 3.5))

Walkthrough

Here’s what went wrong. Look at where Holt’s forecast goes.

That’s a straight line, and it does not stop. Holt estimated a positive trend from the training data — correctly, the series was growing — and then extrapolated it linearly for fifty-two consecutive weeks. By the end of the horizon it’s forecasting a level the store has never reached.

Lecture 1 warned that undamped linear extrapolation stops being credible after about six periods. You are forecasting fifty-two. The model isn’t wrong about the trend; it’s wrong about the trend continuing forever.

Meanwhile SES, the dashed flat line, just says “about here” — and about-here turns out to be a much better guess than a confident straight line pointed at the ceiling.

Try it

Your turn, and it’s genuinely one keyword argument. Uncomment the block and fill in the blank:

holt_damped = Holt(train, damped_trend=True).fit()

That’s the fix. Add it to the table, print the result, and print the estimated phi. Pause here and do it — it’s thirty seconds of typing and you’ll remember it because you typed it.

Holt (damped)    426   351
SES              427   352

Phi comes out at 0.800. Two things here, and the second one is the honest one.

First: damping rescued the model completely. Eleven sixty-six down to four twenty-six. Same model, same data, same trend estimate — one parameter that says “the trend decays as you forecast further out” moved it from worst on the table to best. Phi of 0.8 means each step ahead gets eighty percent of the previous step’s trend, so the forecast bends toward flat instead of running off. That is what phi below 1 means, and it’s the answer to the question in the lab.

Second — and don’t let this slide past — damped Holt beat SES by one unit of RMSE. Four twenty-six against four twenty-seven. That is not a win. On a different held-out year it could easily flip. If you report “the damped model is best” you’re reporting noise as a finding.

What actually happened is that damped Holt and SES are the same answer arrived at two ways. Damping the trend hard enough is close to not having a trend. The honest summary is: on this series, models that keep the forecast near the current level beat models that extrapolate, and the two ways of doing that tie.

1.8 Step 6 — Same store. Different aisle. Opposite answer.

Change one word — HOUSEHOLD becomes FOODS. Same store, same code, same horizon.

y2 = get_series("TX_1", "FOODS")
train2, test2 = y2.iloc[:-N_TEST], y2.iloc[-N_TEST:]

sn2 = y2.shift(52).iloc[-N_TEST:].values
ses2 = SimpleExpSmoothing(train2).fit()

print(f"TX_1 FOODS   seasonal naive RMSE {rmse(test2, sn2):,.0f}")
print(f"TX_1 FOODS   SES            RMSE {rmse(test2, ses2.forecast(N_TEST)):,.0f}")

Notice: the ranking flips. On the household aisle an exponential smoother won comfortably. On the food aisle of the same store, the benchmark wins and SES is about 13% worse.

The reason is visible in one number. For TX_1 HOUSEHOLD the test year sits about 15% above the year before it, so seasonal naive carries a stale level forward and loses badly. For TX_1 FOODS the level barely moves — about 1.5% down — and last year is an excellent guess.

So it is not the store, and it is not the method. It is whether the level moved.

Walkthrough

Change one word. HOUSEHOLD becomes FOODS. Same store, same code, same horizon, same fifty-two weeks held out.

TX_1 FOODS   seasonal naive RMSE 1308
TX_1 FOODS   SES            RMSE 1474

The ranking flips.

On the household aisle, SES beat the benchmark by more than half. On the food aisle of the same store, the benchmark wins and SES is about thirteen percent worse. Nothing changed except which shelf we’re looking at.

So what’s different? It isn’t the store. It isn’t the method — the code is character-for-character identical apart from one string.

It’s visible in one number. For household, the test year sits about fifteen percent above the year before it. The level moved, so seasonal naive carried a stale level forward and lost badly — and a method that tracks the level won.

For foods, the level barely moved — about one and a half percent down. When the level doesn’t move, last year is an excellent guess, and there’s nothing for a level-tracking model to add. It is not the store, and it is not the method. It is whether the level moved.

That’s the sentence to leave with. And notice what it does to the claim we opened with — “always beat a benchmark first” isn’t process hygiene. It’s the only thing that told us the answer was different on these two series. If you’d fit SES on household, seen it win, and rolled it out across the store, you’d have shipped a model that’s thirteen percent worse than doing nothing on the food aisle, and nothing in your workflow would have told you.
