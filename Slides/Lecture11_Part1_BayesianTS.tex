\section{Why Bayesian Time Series}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Why Bayesian Time Series}{%
  Not a more accurate forecast --- a different deliverable.}

% --- Slide: what we have already -----------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{What Every Model So Far Gave You}
  Ten weeks of forecasting, and almost every model returned the same object: \textbf{one number
  per period}. ETS, ARIMA, random forests, XGBoost, LSTMs --- a point forecast, and at best an
  interval derived from asymptotic theory that nobody checked.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  For a planner that is often not enough. \emph{How much stock do I need so the chance of a
  stockout is under 5\%?} is a question about a distribution, and a point forecast cannot answer
  it however accurate it is.

  \begin{keybox}
    A Bayesian time series model returns a \textbf{full predictive distribution for every future
    period}, plus a distribution over each component --- the trend, the seasonality, and the
    noise, each with its own uncertainty.
  \end{keybox}

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \textbf{Set expectations now.} This will not beat XGBoost on RMSE, and on our data it barely
  beats the seasonal-naive benchmark. The argument for it is what it hands you, not where it
  ranks --- and we will hold it to that standard at the end of this lecture.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: what changes -------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Three Things That Become Possible}
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{p{3.5cm}p{8.8cm}}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Priors carry knowledge} & ``Weekly growth is very unlikely to exceed 20\%'' is a
      sentence you can put in the model, rather than hoping the data implies it. \\[3pt]
      \textbf{Components get error bars} & Not just a forecast, but ``how confident am I that
      there is a trend at all?'' --- separately from the seasonality. \\[3pt]
      \textbf{Uncertainty compounds honestly} & Forecast 52 weeks out and the interval widens as
      it should, because the model propagates uncertainty forward instead of assuming it away. \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  That third row is the one people underestimate. A prediction interval from ARIMA widens too,
  but according to a formula derived under assumptions you did not check. Here the widening is a
  consequence of the model you actually wrote down.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{And the price:} it is slower, it needs priors you have to defend, and --- as we will
  see --- it can fail silently in ways a least-squares fit cannot.
\end{frame}

\section{The Structural Decomposition}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{The Structural Decomposition}{%
  Write the series as a sum of parts you can name,
  and put a prior on each.}

% --- Slide: decomposition -------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Structural Time Series: One Equation}
  The whole idea is additive, and you have seen the shape before --- it is the GAM decomposition
  from Lecture 3, with priors attached.
  \[
    y_t \;=\; \underbrace{\mu_t}_{\text{level}} \;+\; \underbrace{s_t}_{\text{seasonality}}
      \;+\; \underbrace{\varepsilon_t}_{\text{noise}},
      \qquad \varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)
  \]
  \vspace{-0.25cm}

  \textbf{Each piece is a thing a business person can name}, and each gets its own posterior.
  ``Demand is growing'' is a statement about $\mu_t$; ``December is our peak'' is a statement
  about $s_t$; ``how noisy is this store?'' is $\sigma$.

  \begin{keybox}
    This is why the approach is called \emph{structural}. You are not fitting a black box to a
    curve --- you are stating what you believe the series is made of, and asking the data how
    much of each part there is.
  \end{keybox}

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  Compare ARIMA, where trend and seasonality are handled by differencing and the parameters are
  correlations rather than quantities anyone can interpret.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: local level --------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{The Level: A Random Walk With a Prior On It}
  The simplest useful trend is a \textbf{local level} --- the series has a level, and the level
  itself drifts:
  \[
    \mu_t = \mu_{t-1} + \eta_t, \qquad \eta_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{\text{level}}^2)
  \]
  \vspace{-0.25cm}

  Read it as a sentence: this week's level is last week's level, plus a shock. And
  $\sigma_{\text{level}}$ --- which you put a prior on --- controls \textbf{how fast the level is
  allowed to move}.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{p{3.4cm}p{8.9cm}}
      \toprule
      Small $\sigma_{\text{level}}$ & A nearly constant level. Rigid, but stable far into the future. \\[3pt]
      Large $\sigma_{\text{level}}$ & A level that chases recent data. Fits well, and forecasts wander. \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.05cm}
  \textbf{That is a familiar trade in new clothing} --- it is the smoothing parameter from
  Lecture 1's exponential smoothing, and the penalty strength from Lecture 6, now expressed as a
  prior you can argue about.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: Fourier seasonality -------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Seasonality as Fourier Terms}
  Fifty-two weekly seasonal parameters would be a lot to estimate. Instead, build seasonality out
  of a few smooth waves:
  \[
    s_t = \sum_{k=1}^{K}\left[a_k \sin\!\left(\tfrac{2\pi k t}{P}\right)
                             + b_k \cos\!\left(\tfrac{2\pi k t}{P}\right)\right]
  \]
  \vspace{-0.25cm}

  $P$ is the period --- for our weekly data, $P = 52$. $K$ is how many waves you allow: $K=1$ is
  a single smooth annual cycle, larger $K$ lets the shape get more detailed. Each $a_k, b_k$ is
  just a coefficient with a Normal prior.

  \begin{keybox}
    \textbf{You have met this exact device twice.} It is how Prophet encodes seasonality in
    Lecture 3, and it is the Fourier feature block we engineered by hand for Homework 4. The
    difference now is that $a_k$ and $b_k$ come back as \emph{distributions}.
  \end{keybox}

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  $K$ trades flexibility against overfitting, exactly like spline knots. We use $K = 3$ --- six
  coefficients standing in for fifty-two.
\end{frame}

\section{Fitting It in PyMC}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Fitting It in PyMC}{%
  The model in code, and the check you run before believing any of it.}

% --- Slide: PyMC ----------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{The Model in PyMC}
  \begin{examplebox}{Local level $+$ Fourier seasonality}
    {\tiny\linespread{1.0}\selectfont
    \texttt{with pm.Model() as model:}\\
    \texttt{\ \ sigma\_level = pm.Exponential("sigma\_level", 10.0)}\\
    \texttt{\ \ z\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ = pm.Normal("z", 0, 1, shape=n)\ \ \ \# non-centred}\\
    \texttt{\ \ level = pm.Deterministic("level", pm.math.cumsum(z * sigma\_level))}\\
    \texttt{\ \ beta\ = pm.Normal("beta", 0, 0.5, shape=2*K)\ \ \ \ \ \ \# Fourier}\\
    \texttt{\ \ sigma = pm.Exponential("sigma", 1.0)}\\[2pt]
    \texttt{\ \ pm.Normal("obs", mu=level + pm.math.dot(X, beta),}\\
    \texttt{\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ sigma=sigma, observed=y\_scaled)}\\
    \texttt{\ \ idata = pm.sample(1500, tune=2000, chains=4, target\_accept=0.95)}
    }
  \end{examplebox}
  Line for line this is the equations above --- \texttt{cumsum} of scaled Normal draws \emph{is}
  a random walk. \textbf{Standardize $y$ first}: these priors assume unit scale, and on raw units
  of 20{,}000 they would be absurdly tight.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: diagnostics failure --------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{The First Fit Failed --- Here Is What That Looks Like}
  Written the obvious way, with \texttt{pm.GaussianRandomWalk}, this model samples badly ---
  even at \texttt{target\_accept=0.95}. Lecture 10's checks caught it:
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{lrrl}
      \toprule
      & \textbf{First attempt} & \textbf{After the fix} & \textbf{Want} \\
      \midrule
      $\hat{R}$ & 1.022 & \textbf{1.002} & $< 1.01$ \\
      ESS (bulk) & 252 & \textbf{2{,}376} & $> 400$ \\
      Divergences & 0 & 0 & $0$ \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{-0.05cm}
  Nothing crashed. The model returned a forecast, and the forecast looked reasonable. Only the
  diagnostics said the chains had not agreed with each other.

  \begin{warningbox}
    \textbf{The fix was reparameterization, not more samples.} Writing the walk as
    \texttt{cumsum(z * sigma)} with $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ --- the \emph{non-centred} form ---
    gives the sampler a geometry it can navigate. BMCP §4.6.1 covers exactly this, and you will
    meet it again next week.
  \end{warningbox}
\end{frame}

\section{Reading the Forecast}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Reading the Forecast}{%
  The point estimate, the interval, and whether either is any use.}

% --- Slide: results --------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Does It Work? The Point Forecast}
  CA\_1 FOODS, 52 weeks held out, forecast from the fitted model.
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{lr}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Model} & \textbf{Test RMSE} \\
      \midrule
      Bayesian structural TS & \textbf{1{,}587} \\
      Seasonal naive (same week last year) & 1{,}660 \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{-0.05cm}
  \textbf{It beats the benchmark by about 4\%.} That is a real improvement and a small one, and
  after nine weeks of this course you should be suspicious of anyone who reports it as a
  triumph.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  It is also worth remembering what this model was given: the series and a calendar. No lag
  features, no price, no SNAP flags, no store identity. The XGBoost model in Homework 4 had 46
  engineered predictors and thirty series to learn from.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \textbf{So the point forecast is not the reason to do this.} The next slide is.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: the interval ----------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{The Interval Is the Product}
  Every forecast comes with a 94\% predictive interval. Two questions about it, and they are
  different questions.
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{p{3.0cm}p{4.0cm}p{5.3cm}}
      \toprule
      & \textbf{Measured} & \textbf{Reading} \\
      \midrule
      \textbf{Calibrated?} & 100\% of weeks fell inside & Not overconfident --- but it should be
      about 94\%, so it is conservative \\[3pt]
      \textbf{Useful?} & Width $\approx$ 11{,}700 units, on a mean of 19{,}700 & About $\pm$30\%.
      Honest, and too wide to order against \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{-0.1cm}
  \textbf{Passing the first does not excuse failing the second.} A model saying ``somewhere
  between 14{,}000 and 26{,}000'' is telling the truth and helping nobody. The cause is
  structural --- a level free to walk for 52 steps drifts, and the model says so. The fix is a
  model with a stronger opinion: a damped level, or covariates that pin it down.

  \vspace{0.05cm}
  \textbf{But notice what just happened.} We could \emph{ask} whether the uncertainty was honest.
  For every point-forecast model in this course, that question had no answer.
\end{frame}

\section{Key Takeaways}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Key Takeaways}{%
  What structural models are for.}

\begin{frame}{Lecture 11 Part 1: Key Takeaways}
  \begin{keybox}
    {\footnotesize
    \begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
      \item A \textbf{structural} model writes the series as named parts --- level, seasonality,
            noise --- and gives each one a prior and a posterior.

      \item The \textbf{local level} is a random walk whose step size $\sigma_{\text{level}}$ you
            put a prior on. Small means rigid, large means it chases the data --- the same trade
            as a smoothing parameter or a penalty.

      \item \textbf{Fourier terms} encode seasonality in a few coefficients instead of 52. Same
            device as Prophet in Lecture 3 and the features you built in Homework 4.

      \item \textbf{Check the sampler before the forecast.} Our first fit had $\hat{R}$ = 1.02
            and ESS = 252 while returning a plausible-looking answer. Reparameterizing fixed it.

      \item The point forecast beat seasonal naive by \textbf{4\%}. That is not the argument.

      \item The interval was \textbf{calibrated but too wide to act on} --- and being able to
            establish that is the actual product.
    \end{enumerate}
    }
  \end{keybox}
  \muted{\itshape Next: hierarchical models --- what to do when you have thirty series and some
  of them barely have data.}
\end{frame}
