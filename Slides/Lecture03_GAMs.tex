\section{Motivation: Beyond Linear, Without Black Boxes}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Motivation: Beyond Linear, Without Black Boxes}{%
  We want flexibility to capture nonlinear relationships
  while still being able to \emph{explain} what the model is doing.}

% --- Slide: The linear / nonlinear tradeoff -----------------------------------
\begin{frame}{The Flexibility--Interpretability Tradeoff}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \textbf{Linear models (ARIMA, regression):}
      {\setbeamertemplate{itemize item}{\pos{$\checkmark$}}
      \begin{itemize}\small\itemsep0pt
        \item Each coefficient has a clear meaning
        \item Stable, well-understood properties
      \end{itemize}}
      {\setbeamertemplate{itemize item}{\negc{$\times$}}
      \begin{itemize}\small\itemsep0pt
        \item Assume a constant slope everywhere
        \item Miss curvature and threshold effects
      \end{itemize}}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \textbf{Fully nonlinear models (trees, neural nets):}
      {\setbeamertemplate{itemize item}{\pos{$\checkmark$}}
      \begin{itemize}\small\itemsep0pt
        \item Capture almost any pattern
        \item High accuracy on complex data
      \end{itemize}}
      {\setbeamertemplate{itemize item}{\negc{$\times$}}
      \begin{itemize}\small\itemsep0pt
        \item Hard to explain any single prediction
        \item Interactions entangle all variables
      \end{itemize}}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vspace{0.3cm}
  \textbf{GAMs sit between the two.} They allow a nonlinear, flexible relationship for each
  variable, but keep the model \textbf{additive} --- so each variable's effect can still be
  plotted and explained on its own. That is the whole bargain, and the rest of this lecture
  is about what it costs.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: The nonlinear marginal effect problem ----------------------------
\begin{frame}{Why Additivity Matters for Interpretation}
  In a non-additive model the effect of one variable depends on the value of another, so you
  cannot describe them separately.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{Concretely.} Suppose the truth is $y = x_1^2 x_2^2 + \varepsilon$. Then
  $\partial y / \partial x_1 = 2x_1 x_2^{2}$ --- it depends on \emph{both} variables. There
  is no answer to ``what does $x_1$ do to $y$?'' that does not begin with ``it depends
  on $x_2$.''
  \begin{examplebox}{GAM Marginal Effects Are Simple}
    In a GAM, $y = f_1(x_1) + f_2(x_2) + \varepsilon$, so the marginal effect of $x_1$ is
    just $f_1'(x_1)$ --- a function of $x_1$ alone, whatever $x_2$ happens to be.\\[3pt]
    \textbf{That is why you can plot each variable's effect separately and hand the plot to
    a stakeholder.}
  \end{examplebox}
\end{frame}

\section{GAM Structure and Smoothing}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{GAM Structure and Smoothing}{%
  Additive nonlinear functions, penalized for wiggliness.}

% --- Slide: GAM definition ---------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{What Is a GAM?}
  \begin{definitionbox}{Generalized Additive Model}
    \vspace{-4pt}
    \[
      y = \alpha + f_1(x_1) + f_2(x_2) + \cdots + f_k(x_k) + \varepsilon
    \]
    \vspace{-6pt}
    \small where each $f_j(\cdot)$ is a \textbf{smooth, nonlinear function} estimated from
    the data \parencite{Wood2017}.
  \end{definitionbox}
  Each $f_j$ is free to be linear, curved, U-shaped, or threshold-like --- the data decides.
  Because the terms are \emph{added} rather than multiplied, the effects stay independent
  and each one can be visualized on its own. Every function is fitted to minimize prediction
  error subject to a smoothness penalty, which is the next slide.

  \vspace{0.2cm}
  \textbf{A retail example.} Model weekly sales as
  $f_1(\text{time})$ for the long-run trend, plus $f_2(\text{week of year})$ for
  seasonality, plus $f_3(\text{unemployment})$ for economic sensitivity. Three curves, each
  plotted and interpreted separately --- and none of them requiring you to hold the others
  fixed to make sense of it.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: what the "Generalized" means -------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{What the ``Generalized'' Actually Means}
  So far every $y$ we have modelled has been continuous and roughly symmetric --- sales in
  dollars, demand in units. For that case an \emph{additive} model is enough.
  The \textbf{G} in GAM is what happens when $y$ is not that kind of variable.
  \begin{definitionbox}{Generalized Additive Model, in full}
    \vspace{-4pt}
    \[
      g\big(\E[y]\big) = \alpha + f_1(x_1) + \cdots + f_k(x_k)
    \]
    \vspace{-6pt}
    \small The additive predictor models a \emph{transformation} of the mean. That
    transformation $g(\cdot)$ is the \textbf{link function}.
  \end{definitionbox}
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{lll}
      \toprule
      \textbf{If $y$ is\ldots} & \textbf{Distribution} & \textbf{Link $g(\cdot)$} \\
      \midrule
      Continuous, symmetric (sales) & Gaussian  & Identity --- $g(\mu)=\mu$ \\
      A count (units, visits, claims) & Poisson   & Log --- keeps the forecast positive \\
      Binary (churn, stockout, yes/no) & Binomial  & Logit --- keeps it inside $[0,1]$ \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.1cm}
  \muted{\footnotesize In \texttt{pyGAM} this is the difference between
  \texttt{LinearGAM}, \texttt{PoissonGAM}, and \texttt{LogisticGAM} --- same additive
  structure, different link. Everything else in this lecture is unchanged.}
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: Smoothness penalty -----------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Controlling Smoothness: The Penalty Term}
  A GAM minimizes a \textbf{penalized} sum of squared errors:
  \[
    \text{PSSE} = \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}_{\text{fit}} +
                  \underbrace{\lambda \int (f''(x))^2\,dx}_{\text{smoothness penalty}}
  \]
  The second derivative $f''(x)$ measures how fast the slope is changing, so a wiggly
  function accumulates a large integral and a smooth one accumulates a small one. The
  penalty therefore charges the model for wiggliness, and $\lambda$ sets the price.

  \vspace{0.2cm}
  The two extremes are worth holding in mind. As \textbf{$\lambda \to 0$} the penalty
  vanishes and the curve is free to chase every point --- overfitting. As
  \textbf{$\lambda \to \infty$} every $f_j$ is forced straight and the GAM collapses back
  into ordinary least squares. Everything useful happens in between, and $\lambda$ is
  chosen by \textbf{cross-validation} or GCV rather than by eye.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \muted{\footnotesize \textbf{Splines} are how $f(x)$ is represented: piecewise polynomials
  joined smoothly at \textbf{knot} points.}
\end{frame}

\section{Prophet for Time Series GAMs}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Prophet for Time Series GAMs}{%
  Facebook's Prophet decomposes a time series into trend, seasonality,
  and holiday effects using a GAM.}

% --- Slide: Prophet overview -------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Prophet: Forecasting at Scale}
  \textcite{TaylorLetham2018} built Prophet for large-scale forecasting at Meta. It is a GAM
  in time-series clothing --- the series is decomposed additively:
  \[
    y(t) = \underbrace{g(t)}_{\text{trend}} + \underbrace{s(t)}_{\text{seasonality}}
          + \underbrace{h(t)}_{\text{holidays}} + \varepsilon_t
  \]
  \textbf{Trend $g(t)$} is piecewise linear or logistic, with \emph{changepoints} where the
  slope is allowed to shift. \textbf{Seasonality $s(t)$} is a Fourier series, which is how
  Prophet handles weekly and yearly cycles simultaneously. \textbf{Holidays $h(t)$} are
  irregular events you supply yourself --- Black Friday, a product launch, a lockdown.

  \vspace{0.2cm}
  The design goals were practical rather than statistical: one fit-and-forecast call, robust
  to missing data and outliers, and interpretable because every component can be plotted on
  its own. That is why it spread so quickly among analysts who are not statisticians.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \muted{\footnotesize\itshape \texttt{changepoint\_prior\_scale} controls trend flexibility:
  high adapts quickly and risks overfitting; low keeps the trend stable.}
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: Prophet code -----------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Prophet: Code and Workflow}
  \begin{examplebox}{Prophet on Retail Sales}
    {\scriptsize
    \texttt{from prophet import Prophet}\\
    \texttt{df = pd.DataFrame(\{'ds': dates, 'y': sales\})}\\
    \texttt{m = Prophet(changepoint\_prior\_scale=0.05, yearly\_seasonality=True).fit(df)}\\
    \texttt{future = m.make\_future\_dataframe(periods=12, freq='M')}\\
    \texttt{forecast = m.predict(future);\ \ m.plot\_components(forecast)}
    }
  \end{examplebox}
  \texttt{plot\_components} is the call that matters here: it draws the trend with its
  changepoints, the yearly wave, and the day-of-week pattern as separate panels --- the
  additive structure made visible.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{Reach for Prophet} on seasonal series with known holiday effects, especially when
  someone will ask you to explain the forecast. \textbf{Reach for pyGAM} when you have many
  correlated external predictors instead.
\end{frame}

\section{pyGAM for General Additive Modeling}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{pyGAM for General Additive Modeling}{%
  When you have multiple predictors and want flexible nonlinear effects
  for each.}

% --- Slide: pyGAM overview ---------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{pyGAM: Flexible Additive Modeling}
  \texttt{pyGAM} fits a GAM with a smooth function you specify per predictor:
  \begin{examplebox}{pyGAM Example}
    {\footnotesize
    \texttt{from pygam import LinearGAM, s, f, l}\\[2pt]
    \texttt{gam = LinearGAM(s(0) + s(1) + s(2) + f(3))}\\
    \texttt{gam = gam.gridsearch(X, y)}\\[2pt]
    \texttt{pdep, confi = gam.partial\_dependence(term=0, width=0.95)}\\
    \texttt{y\_hat = gam.predict(X\_new)}
    }
  \end{examplebox}
  The three term types are the whole vocabulary: \texttt{s(j)} is a smooth spline of
  feature $j$ --- the flexible nonlinear effect; \texttt{l(j)} forces a straight line, which
  is worth using when theory says the effect should be linear; and \texttt{f(j)} handles a
  binary or categorical feature.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \texttt{gridsearch} then tunes $\lambda$ by cross-validation --- though you should still
  look at the resulting curves and ask whether they are plausible.
\end{frame}

\section{Key Takeaways and Roadmap}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Key Takeaways and Roadmap}{%
  GAMs: flexible, interpretable, and underutilized in business forecasting.}

% --- Slide: GAMs vs. alternatives --------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{GAMs vs.\ Other Methods: When to Choose Each}
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{lllll}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Method} & \textbf{Nonlinear} & \textbf{Interactions} & \textbf{Interpretable} & \textbf{Use case} \\
      \midrule
      ARIMA / ETS       & No  & No     & High   & Univariate time series \\
      Linear regression & No  & Manual & High   & Few predictors \\
      \textbf{GAM}      & Yes & No     & High   & Few predictors, explain effects \\
      Random Forest     & Yes & Yes    & Medium & Many predictors, tabular \\
      XGBoost           & Yes & Yes    & Low    & Best accuracy, tabular \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  The column that decides it is usually \textbf{Interactions}. A GAM is additive by
  construction, so if the effect of price genuinely depends on whether it is a holiday week,
  a GAM cannot represent that and a tree can.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{Choose a GAM when} you must explain each predictor's effect, when those effects
  are curved rather than straight, when a time series has complex trend and seasonality
  (reach for Prophet), and when interpretability matters more than the last few points of
  accuracy. That last clause is the honest one --- on tabular data, XGBoost will usually
  win on RMSE, and Lecture 6 is where we take that seriously.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: Key takeaways ---------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Lecture 3: Key Takeaways}
  \begin{keybox}
    {\small
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item \textbf{GAMs} offer nonlinear flexibility while maintaining interpretability:
            $y = \alpha + f_1(x_1) + f_2(x_2) + \cdots + f_k(x_k) + \varepsilon$.
            Because the model is additive, each variable's effect can be visualized
            and explained independently.

      \item \textbf{Smoothness penalty} (PSSE) prevents overfitting.
            The penalty $\lambda$ controls the trade-off between fit and smoothness.
            It is chosen by cross-validation.

      \item \textbf{Prophet} decomposes a time series into trend, seasonality, and
            holiday effects using a GAM. Ideal for business time series with
            seasonal patterns and known irregular events.

      \item \textbf{pyGAM} fits a general GAM with any combination of predictors.
            Use \texttt{s()} for smooth effects, \texttt{l()} for linear,
            \texttt{f()} for categorical. \texttt{gridsearch} tunes $\lambda$ automatically.

      \item Partial dependence plots make results \textbf{actionable for stakeholders}:
            show clearly how each variable affects the forecast.
    \end{enumerate}
    }
  \end{keybox}
  \muted{\itshape Next: Decision Trees --- moving to tree-based methods
  that capture interactions automatically.}
\end{frame}
