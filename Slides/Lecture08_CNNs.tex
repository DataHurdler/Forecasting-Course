\section{Convolution: The Core Idea}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Convolution: The Core Idea}{%
  Look at a small window, and look for the same thing everywhere.
  Two ideas, and they are the whole architecture.}

% --- Slide: Why convolution --------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Why Convolution?}
  Start with why a fully connected layer fails on structured input. A $224 \times 224$ image has
  50{,}176 pixels, so a single hidden layer of 1{,}000 neurons needs \textbf{50 million weights}
  --- for one layer. Lecture 7's network had 22{,}657 parameters in total.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  Worse than the cost is the waste. That layer ignores \textbf{spatial structure}: it has no
  notion that neighboring pixels are related and distant ones usually are not. And it does not
  \textbf{generalize position} --- a pattern learned in the top-left corner is stored separately
  from the same pattern in the bottom-right.

  \begin{keybox}
    A convolutional layer fixes both with two ideas. \textbf{Local connectivity:} each neuron
    sees only a small \textbf{receptive field}. \textbf{Weight sharing:} the same filter is
    applied at every position. Far fewer parameters, and a pattern learned anywhere is
    recognized everywhere.
  \end{keybox}

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  The analogy that makes it stick: a reader does not memorize the letter ``A'' separately for
  every position on the page. They learn it once and apply it everywhere. A filter does exactly
  that.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: How convolution works --------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{How a Convolutional Layer Works}
  For a sequence, a 1D convolution slides a filter of length $K$ along the series and takes a dot
  product at each position:
  \[
    (f * g)[t] = \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} f[k] \cdot g[t - k]
  \]
  \vspace{-0.25cm}
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{p{2.3cm}p{10.1cm}}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Kernel size} & Filter length $K$. Larger means a wider receptive field --- more
      context per output, and more parameters. \\[2pt]
      \textbf{Stride} & How far the filter moves between positions. Stride 2 halves the output
      length. \\[2pt]
      \textbf{Padding} & Zeros at the edges. \texttt{padding='same'} keeps output length equal to
      input length. \\[2pt]
      \textbf{Filters} & How many different patterns to learn. This becomes the number of output
      channels. \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  Each filter learns one \textbf{local pattern} --- an edge, a spike, a seasonal dip. Its output
  is a \textbf{feature map} whose high values mark where that pattern was found.
\end{frame}

\section{Pooling and Dropout}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Pooling and Dropout}{%
  Pooling shrinks the feature map and buys robustness ---
  at a price that matters more for forecasting than for images.}

% --- Slide: Pooling ----------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Pooling: Downsampling Feature Maps}
  \textbf{Pooling} reduces a feature map by summarizing each small region with a single number.
  Max pooling takes the largest value in the window; average pooling takes the mean. A
  $2\times2$ max pool with stride 2 quarters the number of values.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  Two things are bought. \textbf{Fewer parameters downstream}, because later layers see a smaller
  input. And \textbf{translation invariance}: shift the input slightly and the pooled output
  barely changes, so the network recognizes a cat whether it sits left or right of center.

  \begin{warningbox}
    \textbf{Translation invariance is a feature for images and a problem for forecasting.} If a
    demand spike moved three weeks later, that is not a nuisance to be smoothed away --- it is
    the thing you are trying to predict. Pooling deliberately discards \emph{where} in the window
    a pattern occurred, and for a time series the position is often the signal.
  \end{warningbox}

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  Hold that objection. We measure exactly what it costs later in this lecture.
\end{frame}

\section{CNN Architecture Evolution}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{CNN Architecture Evolution}{%
  Thirty years of architectures, and three ideas that survived.}

% --- Slide: LeNet to VGG -----------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{LeNet to VGG: Depth and Small Filters}
  \textbf{LeNet-5} \parencite{LeCun1998} established the template in 1998: alternating
  convolution and pooling layers, then fully connected layers at the end. It had roughly 60{,}000
  parameters and read handwritten digits on cheques.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{VGG} \parencite{Simonyan2015} scaled that idea up in 2014 and made one sharp
  simplification: use \emph{only} $3\times3$ filters, but stack many of them. Two stacked
  $3\times3$ layers see the same receptive field as one $5\times5$, with fewer parameters and an
  extra nonlinearity in between.

  \begin{keybox}
    \textbf{The lesson that carried:} depth built from small filters beats width built from large
    ones. It is cheaper in parameters and more expressive, and it is why almost every modern
    architecture is deep and narrow rather than shallow and wide.
  \end{keybox}

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  VGG's cost was its size --- around 138 million parameters, most of them in the final fully
  connected layers. That is the problem the next two architectures attacked.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: Inception and ResNet ---------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Inception and ResNet: Depth That Trains}
  \textbf{Inception} \parencite{Szegedy2015} asked why a layer should commit to one filter size.
  Its module runs $1\times1$, $3\times3$ and $5\times5$ convolutions \emph{in parallel} and
  concatenates the results, letting the network choose the scale. $1\times1$ convolutions reduce
  channel count first, keeping the cost down.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{ResNet} \parencite{He2016} solved a different problem. Past a certain depth, deeper
  networks got \emph{worse} --- not from overfitting, but because gradients degraded on their way
  back through many layers. The fix is a \textbf{residual} or skip connection:
  \[
    \mathbf{y} = \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}) + \mathbf{x}
  \]
  \vspace{-0.3cm}

  Each block learns the \emph{difference} from its input rather than a whole new representation,
  and the $+\,\mathbf{x}$ gives the gradient a direct path backward. That single change made
  networks of 100+ layers trainable, and skip connections now appear in nearly every deep
  architecture --- including the Transformers in Lecture 9.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: what the image architectures taught -------------------------------
\begin{frame}{What Carries Over to Time Series}
  These architectures were built for images, on problems that look nothing like weekly demand
  forecasting. Three of their ideas transfer anyway.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{p{3.6cm}p{8.8cm}}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Small stacked filters} & A width-3 filter twice sees six weeks of context with far
      fewer weights than one width-6 filter. \\[3pt]
      \textbf{Skip connections} & Same fix for the same problem --- gradients that fade through
      depth. \\[3pt]
      \textbf{Learned features} & You stop hand-engineering lag windows and let filters find the
      local shapes that matter. \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \textbf{One idea does not transfer, and it is the one we flagged:} pooling for translation
  invariance. An image classifier should not care where the cat is. A forecaster usually should
  care where the spike was.
\end{frame}

\section{1D CNNs for Time Series}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{1D CNNs for Time Series}{%
  The same machinery, one dimension, and an honest measurement.}

% --- Slide: why 1D CNN -------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Why Use a 1D CNN for Forecasting?}
  A 1D CNN takes a \textbf{window} of the recent past --- say 26 weeks --- across several
  channels, and predicts the next value. Each channel is one series: units, SNAP days, events,
  price.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  The appeal is that you stop choosing lags. Rather than deciding that lag 1, lag 4 and lag 52
  matter, you hand the model the whole window and let the filters learn which local shapes
  predict what comes next.

  \begin{keybox}
    \textbf{Two genuine advantages.} It is far cheaper than a fully connected network on the same
    window --- weight sharing again. And it handles \emph{many parallel series} naturally, since
    the same filters apply to every store-category at once.
  \end{keybox}

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \textbf{The catch is the receptive field.} Stacked width-5 and width-3 filters see about seven
  weeks of context. Annual seasonality is 52 weeks away, and no reasonable stack of small filters
  reaches that far. Either you add dilation, or you feed the annual lag in as its own channel.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: pipeline ---------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{1D CNN Forecasting Pipeline in PyTorch}
  \begin{examplebox}{Window in, one forecast out}
    {\tiny\linespread{1.2}\selectfont
    \texttt{import torch.nn as nn}\\[2pt]
    \texttt{model = nn.Sequential(}\\
    \texttt{\ \ \ \ nn.Conv1d(n\_channels, 32, kernel\_size=5), nn.ReLU(),}\\
    \texttt{\ \ \ \ nn.Conv1d(32, 64, kernel\_size=3),\ \ \ \ \ \ \ nn.ReLU(),}\\
    \texttt{\ \ \ \ nn.AdaptiveAvgPool1d(1),\ \ \ \# collapses the time axis}\\
    \texttt{\ \ \ \ nn.Flatten(),}\\
    \texttt{\ \ \ \ nn.Linear(64, 1))}
    }
  \end{examplebox}
  Input shape is \texttt{(batch, channels, time)} --- note that channels come \emph{before} time,
  which is the transpose of how the data naturally sits in a dataframe.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  Everything from Lecture 7 still applies: Adam with weight decay, \texttt{zero\_grad()} every
  batch, \texttt{shuffle=False}, and a split by time rather than at random.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \muted{\footnotesize\itshape \texttt{AdaptiveAvgPool1d(1)} averages across the whole time axis.
  Watch what that does on the next slide.}
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: does it work -----------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Does It Actually Work?}
  Fitted on our weekly panel --- 26-week windows, four channels, 30 store-category series, the
  same test block as Homework 4.
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{lrr}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Model} & \textbf{Test RMSE} & \textbf{Parameters} \\
      \midrule
      LASSO (Homework 4) & \textbf{744} & 46 \\
      XGBoost (Homework 4) & 781 & --- \\
      Random forest (Homework 3) & 899 & --- \\
      \midrule
      1D CNN, flatten head & 1{,}032 & 8{,}161 \\
      1D CNN, global max pool & 1{,}418 & 6{,}945 \\
      1D CNN, global average pool & 1{,}534 & 6{,}945 \\
      \midrule
      Seasonal naive & 2{,}152 & 0 \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{-0.2cm}
  \textbf{Every CNN variant loses to a 46-coefficient linear model} --- a seven-week receptive
  field cannot see the annual lag LASSO was handed directly.

  \vspace{0.05cm}
  And swapping average pooling for \texttt{Flatten}, which keeps \emph{where} each pattern
  occurred, is worth about \textbf{500 RMSE} --- three seeds each, against a spread of about 80.
\end{frame}

\section{Key Takeaways}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Key Takeaways}{%
  What convolution is for, and what it is not for.}

% --- Slide: architecture summary ---------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{CNN Architecture Summary}
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{llp{6.6cm}}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Architecture} & \textbf{Year} & \textbf{The idea that survived} \\
      \midrule
      LeNet-5 & 1998 & Conv--pool--conv--pool--dense: the template \\
      VGG & 2014 & Only $3\times3$ filters, stacked deep \\
      Inception & 2014 & Parallel filter sizes; $1\times1$ to cut channels \\
      ResNet & 2015 & Skip connections make real depth trainable \\
      1D CNN & --- & The same machinery along a time axis \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{Reach for a 1D CNN} when local shape matters more than exact position, when you have
  many parallel series, or as a cheap feature extractor feeding something else.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \textbf{Do not reach for one} when the dominant signal is at a long lag, when you have well
  engineered tabular features already, or when a stakeholder needs to read the model. On the
  evidence above, that covers most of what we forecast in this course.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: Key takeaways ----------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Lecture 8: Key Takeaways}
  \begin{keybox}
    {\footnotesize
    \begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
      \item \textbf{Convolution} is local connectivity plus weight sharing. A pattern is learned
            once and detected everywhere, at a fraction of the parameters.

      \item \textbf{Pooling} buys translation invariance --- which an image classifier wants and
            a forecaster usually does not. Measured cost here: about 500 RMSE.

      \item The architecture history reduces to three transferable ideas: \textbf{small filters
            stacked deep}, \textbf{skip connections} for trainable depth, and \textbf{learned}
            rather than hand-engineered features.

      \item A \textbf{1D CNN} takes a window across channels and predicts the next step. Its
            weakness is the \textbf{receptive field}: seven weeks of context cannot see an annual
            lag.

      \item On our panel every CNN variant \textbf{lost to a 46-coefficient LASSO}. Architecture
            has to fit the problem; it is not a ladder you climb.
    \end{enumerate}
    }
  \end{keybox}
  \muted{\itshape Next: RNNs and LSTMs --- architectures built to carry state through time,
  which is what the receptive field could not do.}
\end{frame}
