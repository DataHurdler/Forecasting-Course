\section{From Single Trees to Ensembles}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{From Single Trees to Ensembles}{%
  Averaging many imperfect models can outperform any single model.}

% --- Slide: Wisdom of crowds -------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{The Wisdom of Crowds}
  A single expert can be wrong. Average many \emph{independent} estimates and the errors tend
  to cancel.
  \begin{examplebox}{Classic Example: Guessing the Ox's Weight}
    At a 1907 county fair, 800 people guessed the weight of an ox. No individual was very
    accurate --- but the \textbf{average of all 800 guesses} came within one pound of the
    true weight.
  \end{examplebox}

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  The same principle transfers directly to models. A single decision tree is unstable and
  noisy --- that was Lecture 4's closing complaint. Train many trees on different versions of
  the data and average them, and the noise largely cancels while the signal survives.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{Ensembles work because independent errors cancel.} And that word is doing real
  work: the \emph{more independent} the individual predictions, the more variance averaging
  removes. Hold onto it --- the entire difference between bagging and random forests turns on
  it.
\end{frame}

% =============================================================================
\section{Bagging: Bootstrap Aggregating}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Bagging: Bootstrap Aggregating}{%
  Train many trees on bootstrap samples; average their predictions.}

% --- Slide: What is bagging --------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Bagging: The Big Idea}
  \textbf{Problem from Lecture 4:} a single decision tree has very high variance --- change a
  few training points and you get a completely different tree.

  \textbf{Bagging solution \parencite{Breiman1996}:}
  \begin{enumerate}\small\itemsep0pt
    \item Draw $B$ \textbf{bootstrap samples} (with replacement, same size as the original)
    \item Fit one deep decision tree on each
    \item \textbf{Average} all $B$ predictions:
          $\hat{y}_{\text{bag}} = \tfrac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} \hat{f}^{(b)}(\mathbf{x})$
  \end{enumerate}

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \textbf{Why it works.} The variance of the averaged forecast is
  \[
    \Var(\bar{f}) = \rho\sigma^2 + \tfrac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2,
  \]
  where $\rho$ is the pairwise correlation between tree predictions. The second term shrinks
  as $B$ grows --- so more trees never hurts. But the first term does not move at all.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \textbf{That leaves a floor at $\rho\sigma^2$}, set by how alike the trees are rather than
  by how many you grow. Averaging a thousand nearly identical trees buys you almost nothing,
  and that is the problem the next section solves.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: Out-of-bag error --------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Out-of-Bag (OOB) Error: Free Cross-Validation}
  Each bootstrap sample draws $n$ observations \emph{with replacement}, so it contains about
  \textbf{63\%} of the distinct training points. The remaining 37\% are \textbf{out-of-bag}
  for that tree --- a free test set, different for every tree:
  \begin{enumerate}\small\itemsep0pt
    \item For each observation $i$, collect predictions only from the trees where $i$ was
          out-of-bag
    \item Average them to get $\hat{y}_i^{\text{OOB}}$
    \item Compute the RMSE across all observations
  \end{enumerate}

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  Every one of those predictions came from a tree that never saw the observation --- so the
  OOB RMSE is an honest out-of-sample estimate, and it lands close to 5-fold CV without
  refitting anything. In \texttt{sklearn} it is one argument:
  \texttt{RandomForestRegressor(oob\_score=True)}.
  \begin{warningbox}
    Below about 1{,}000 observations the OOB estimate gets noisy, because each observation is
    out-of-bag for too few trees. On a weekly business series --- a few hundred rows --- use
    proper walk-forward CV instead.
  \end{warningbox}
\end{frame}

\section{Random Forests: Decorrelating the Trees}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Random Forests: Decorrelating the Trees}{%
  Restrict each split to a random subset of features so trees are less alike.}

% --- Slide: The correlation problem ------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{The Problem Bagging Does Not Fully Solve}
  Recall the variance of the averaged forecast:
  $\Var(\bar{f}) = \rho\sigma^2 + \tfrac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2$. More trees shrink the second
  term to nothing. The first term never moves.
  \begin{warningbox}
    \textbf{The correlation problem.} If one feature dominates --- and in forecasting it
    always does, because last year's value is the best single predictor --- then nearly every
    tree splits on it at the root. The trees end up near-identical, $\rho$ stays large, and
    bagging buys you very little.
  \end{warningbox}

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{The Random Forest fix \parencite{Breiman2001}} is almost absurdly simple: at each
  split, consider only a random subset of $m < p$ features, and take the best split among
  \emph{those}.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  Sometimes the dominant feature is not in the candidate set, so the tree is forced to find
  its second-best story. Across hundreds of trees that produces genuinely different
  structures --- $\rho$ falls, and the floor falls with it. You deliberately make each
  individual tree \emph{worse} in order to make the ensemble better.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: RF hyperparameters -----------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Random Forest Hyperparameters}
  \begin{center}
    {\footnotesize
    \begin{tabular}{p{3.0cm}p{2.8cm}p{1.8cm}p{4.0cm}}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Parameter} & \textbf{sklearn name} & \textbf{Default} & \textbf{Effect} \\
      \midrule
      Number of trees & \texttt{n\_estimators} & 100 & More = lower variance; plateaus $\sim$500 \\
      Max features/split & \texttt{max\_features} & \texttt{'sqrt'} & Lower = more decorrelated \\
      Max depth & \texttt{max\_depth} & None & Limit only to reduce memory \\
      Min samples/leaf & \texttt{min\_samples\_leaf} & 1 & Higher = smoother predictions \\
      OOB score & \texttt{oob\_score} & False & \texttt{True} gives a free CV estimate \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \textbf{Start with} \texttt{n\_estimators=500}, \texttt{max\_features='sqrt'},
  \texttt{min\_samples\_leaf=5}, \texttt{oob\_score=True}. Note how few genuinely need
  tuning --- \texttt{max\_features} is the dial controlling $\rho$, and the rest is close to
  fire-and-forget. That is most of why random forests make a good default.

  \muted{\footnotesize Set \texttt{random\_state=42}: a forest is random twice over, in the
  bootstrap samples and in the feature subsets.}
\end{frame}

\section{Feature Importance and Interpretation}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Feature Importance and Interpretation}{%
  Which predictors matter most? RF gives a data-driven answer.}

% --- Slide: Feature importance -----------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Random Forest Feature Importance \parencite{Molnar2022}}
  \begin{definitionbox}{Mean Decrease in Impurity (MDI)}
    \small For each feature $j$, average the total reduction in node impurity across every
    split on $j$, weighted by the fraction of observations reaching that node. Normalize to
    sum to 1.
  \end{definitionbox}
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{lc}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Feature (CA\_1 FOODS, weekly)} & \textbf{Importance} \\
      \midrule
      \texttt{units\_lag4}  & 0.45 \\
      \texttt{units\_lag1}  & 0.18 \\
      \texttt{units\_lag52} & 0.11 \\
      \texttt{snap\_days}   & 0.08 \\
      \texttt{units\_lag2}  & 0.04 \\
      \texttt{avg\_price}   & 0.04 \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  Read it as a screening device, not an explanation. \textbf{MDI is biased} toward features
  with many split points. \texttt{permutation\_importance} --- shuffle a column, see how much
  accuracy falls --- is the honest alternative.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: Python implementation -----------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Random Forest in Python}
  \begin{examplebox}{sklearn Code Template}
    {\scriptsize
    \texttt{from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestRegressor}\\
    \texttt{rf = RandomForestRegressor(n\_estimators=500, max\_features='sqrt',}\\
    \texttt{\ \ \ min\_samples\_leaf=5, oob\_score=True, random\_state=42).fit(X\_tr, y\_tr)}\\
    \texttt{print(rf.oob\_score\_);\ \ importances = rf.feature\_importances\_}
    }
  \end{examplebox}
  \begin{center}
    {\footnotesize
    \begin{tabular}{lc}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Model (CA\_1 FOODS, 52-week test)} & \textbf{Test RMSE} \\
      \midrule
      Seasonal na\"{i}ve   & 1,660 \\
      Single deep tree     & 1,415 \\
      \textbf{Random Forest} & \textbf{1,020} \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  The forest cuts RMSE roughly \textbf{28\%} against a single deep tree, and is the first
  method in this course to beat the seasonal naive benchmark on this series.

  \muted{\footnotesize What changed is not a better model of the series but a richer feature
  set --- lags, price, and SNAP days are information ETS and ARIMA never had.}
\end{frame}

\section{Gradient Boosting}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Gradient Boosting}{%
  Instead of averaging independent trees, fit each new tree to the errors
  of all previous trees.}

% --- Slide: Boosting vs. bagging ---------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Boosting vs.\ Bagging: The Core Difference}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \textbf{Bagging (Random Forest):}
      \begin{itemize}\small
        \item Trees built \textbf{in parallel}, independently
        \item Each tree tries to predict $y$ directly
        \item Average reduces variance
        \item Adding more trees does not reduce bias
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \textbf{Boosting:}
      \begin{itemize}\small
        \item Trees built \textbf{sequentially}
        \item Each tree fits the \emph{errors} of the previous ensemble
        \item Adding more trees reduces both bias AND variance (carefully)
        \item Risk: can overfit if trees are too deep or too many
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \begin{examplebox}{Analogy: Correcting a Forecast Iteratively}
    \small
    Forecast 1: ``Sales will be \$50,000.'' \quad Actual: \$60,000. \quad Error: $+$\$10,000.\\[2pt]
    Forecast 2: ``The first model was short by \$10,000.''\\
    Combined prediction: \$50,000 $+$ \$10,000 $=$ \textbf{\$60,000}.\\[2pt]
    Each stage corrects the remaining error from all previous stages.
  \end{examplebox}
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: Boosting algorithm -----------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{The Gradient Boosting Algorithm}
  \begin{definitionbox}{Gradient Boosting (for squared-error loss)}
    {\footnotesize
    Initialize $F_0(\mathbf{x}) = \bar{y}$. For $b = 1, \ldots, B$:
    \begin{enumerate}\itemsep0pt\topsep0pt
      \item Compute residuals: $r_i^{(b)} = y_i - F_{b-1}(\mathbf{x}_i)$
      \item Fit a shallow tree $T_b$ to $\{(\mathbf{x}_i, r_i^{(b)})\}$
      \item Update: $F_b = F_{b-1} + \eta\, T_b$
    \end{enumerate}
    Final prediction: $\hat{y} = F_B(\mathbf{x})$
    }
  \end{definitionbox}
  \vspace{-0.2cm}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
      \textbf{The learning rate $\eta$:}
      \begin{itemize}\footnotesize\itemsep0pt
        \item Small $\eta$ (0.01--0.1): tiny step per tree ---
              requires more trees but more robust
        \item Large $\eta$ ($> 0.3$): fast but risks overshooting
        \item \textbf{Rule:} smaller $\eta$ + more trees + early stopping
              typically wins
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
      \begin{keybox}
        \footnotesize Trees are \textbf{shallow} (depth 3--6).
        Each corrects a small fraction of the total error.
        The ensemble slowly converges.\\[2pt]
        \textbf{Warning:} always use \textbf{early stopping} ---
        without it, boosting will overfit.
      \end{keybox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\section{XGBoost}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{XGBoost}{%
  Three improvements over vanilla boosting: second-order optimization,
  explicit regularization, and column subsampling.}

% --- Slide: XGBoost innovations -----------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{XGBoost: Three Advances \parencite{Chen2016}}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.56\textwidth}
      \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Newton step (2nd-order optimization):}
              Uses the gradient \emph{and} curvature (Hessian)
              of the loss function to compute more precise tree-fitting directions.
              Converges faster than first-order boosting.

        \item \textbf{Explicit regularization:}
              Penalizes the number of leaves ($\gamma$) and the magnitude
              of leaf weights ($\lambda$) directly in the objective.
              Guards against overfitting without relying on early stopping alone.

        \item \textbf{Column subsampling:}
              Like Random Forests, randomly samples features per tree and per split.
              Reduces correlation and improves generalization.
      \end{enumerate}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.40\textwidth}
      \begin{keybox}
        XGBoost dominated Kaggle competitions from 2014--2018.
        It consistently outperforms vanilla gradient boosting
        on tabular data.
      \end{keybox}
      \vspace{0.2cm}
      \textbf{Other popular options:}
      \begin{itemize}\small
        \item \texttt{LightGBM} --- faster on large datasets
        \item \texttt{CatBoost} --- handles categorical features natively
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: XGBoost key hyperparameters --------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{XGBoost: Key Hyperparameters}
  \begin{center}
    {\footnotesize
    \begin{tabular}{p{2.6cm}p{2.8cm}p{1.8cm}p{4.0cm}}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Parameter} & \textbf{XGB name} & \textbf{Typical} & \textbf{Effect} \\
      \midrule
      Number of trees & \texttt{n\_estimators} & 500--2000 & More + small $\eta$ = better (use early stopping) \\
      Learning rate & \texttt{learning\_rate} & 0.01--0.1 & Smaller = more robust; needs more trees \\
      Tree depth & \texttt{max\_depth} & 3--6 & Shallow preferred; depth $>$6 risks overfit \\
      Row subsample & \texttt{subsample} & 0.7--0.9 & Fraction of training rows per tree \\
      Col.\ subsample & \texttt{colsample\_bytree} & 0.7--0.9 & Feature fraction per tree (like RF) \\
      L2 leaf penalty & \texttt{reg\_lambda} & 1 & Ridge on leaf weights; stabilizes \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}
  \begin{keybox}
    \small \textbf{Practical starting point:}
    \texttt{learning\_rate=0.05}, \texttt{max\_depth=4},
    \texttt{n\_estimators=1000} with early stopping on validation RMSE.
    Tune \texttt{max\_depth} and \texttt{subsample} via \texttt{TimeSeriesSplit}.
  \end{keybox}
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: XGBoost Python ---------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{XGBoost in Python}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
      \begin{examplebox}{Code Template}
        {\scriptsize
        \texttt{model = xgb.XGBRegressor(}\\
        \texttt{  n\_estimators=1000,}\\
        \texttt{  learning\_rate=0.05,}\\
        \texttt{  max\_depth=4,}\\
        \texttt{  subsample=0.8,}\\
        \texttt{  colsample\_bytree=0.8,}\\
        \texttt{  early\_stopping\_rounds=50,}\\
        \texttt{  random\_state=42)}\\
        \texttt{model.fit(}\\
        \texttt{  X\_train, y\_train,}\\
        \texttt{  eval\_set=[(X\_val, y\_val)],}\\
        \texttt{  verbose=False)}
        }
      \end{examplebox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
      \textbf{Cross-validation for time series:}
      \begin{itemize}\footnotesize\itemsep0pt
        \item Use \texttt{TimeSeriesSplit} with the sklearn API
        \item Reserve last 20\% as final test set
        \item \texttt{early\_stopping\_rounds=50}: stops if val.\ RMSE stalls for 50 rounds
      \end{itemize}
      \muted{\footnotesize Typical RSXFS result:
      XGBoost RMSE $\approx$ 1,500--1,700 (vs.\ RF $\approx$ 1,800).}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\section{Key Takeaways and Roadmap}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Key Takeaways and Roadmap}{%
  Bagging reduces variance; boosting reduces bias. Together they cover
  most of what tabular forecasting needs.}

% --- Slide: Which method when -------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Tree Methods: Which One When}
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{lllll}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Method} & \textbf{Training} & \textbf{Interpretable} & \textbf{Tuning} & \textbf{Typical accuracy} \\
      \midrule
      Decision Tree & Single tree & High & Low & Baseline \\
      Random Forest & Parallel ($B$ trees) & Medium & Low & Good \\
      XGBoost & Sequential ($B$ trees) & Low & Higher & Best \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \textbf{Random Forest strengths:}
      {\setbeamertemplate{itemize item}{\pos{$\checkmark$}}
      \begin{itemize}\small\itemsep0pt
        \item Robust --- hard to overfit badly
        \item Minimal tuning; good out-of-the-box
        \item OOB error gives free cross-validation
      \end{itemize}}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \textbf{Shared limitation:}
      {\setbeamertemplate{itemize item}{\negc{$\times$}}
      \begin{itemize}\small\itemsep0pt
        \item Neither can extrapolate beyond the training range ---
              a hard constraint for trending series
      \end{itemize}}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \begin{keybox}
    \small \textbf{Practical order:} start with a Random Forest --- it is hard to make
    catastrophically wrong. Move to XGBoost when you have the time to tune and
    need the last few points of accuracy.
  \end{keybox}
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: Key takeaways ---------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Lecture 5: Key Takeaways}
  \begin{keybox}
    {\footnotesize
    \begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
      \item \textbf{Ensembles} work because independent errors cancel.
            Averaging many imperfect models beats any single model.

      \item \textbf{Bagging} averages $B$ trees fit to bootstrap samples;
            variance falls as $B$ grows but floors at $\rho\sigma^2$.
            \textbf{Random Forests} subsample features at each split to cut
            $\rho$ --- the key gain over plain bagging.

      \item \textbf{OOB error} is free cross-validation: each observation sits
            out $\approx$37\% of trees, giving an unbiased generalization estimate.

      \item \textbf{Boosting} inverts the idea --- trees are built sequentially,
            each fitting the ensemble's remaining errors. Bagging attacks
            variance; boosting attacks bias. \textbf{XGBoost} adds a Newton step,
            penalties on leaf count and weights, and column subsampling.

      \item Tune by cross-validation, always with \texttt{TimeSeriesSplit} ---
            a random split leaks the future into the past --- and pair boosting
            with \textbf{early stopping}.
    \end{enumerate}
    }
  \end{keybox}
  \muted{\itshape Next: Regularization \& Model Selection ---
  choosing among many predictors by shrinking coefficients rather than counting them.}
\end{frame}
