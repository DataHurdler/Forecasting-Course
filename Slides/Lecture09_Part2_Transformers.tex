\section{What Recurrence Could Not Do}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{What Recurrence Could Not Do}{%
  Two constraints that are built into reading a sequence
  one step at a time.}

% --- Slide: limits of recurrence ---------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Two Problems Built Into Recurrence}
  Part 1 ended with a working recurrent forecaster. Both of its remaining problems come from the
  same source --- it reads strictly left to right.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{The information bottleneck.} Everything the model knows about step 3 must survive being
  squeezed through every hidden state between 3 and $T$. An LSTM's gates make that survival more
  likely, but the whole history is still compressed into one fixed-size vector.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{The sequential bottleneck.} Step $t$ cannot be computed until step $t-1$ is finished.
  That is a hard constraint: a 1{,}000-step sequence takes 1{,}000 sequential operations no
  matter how many GPUs you own. Convolutions parallelize; recurrence does not.

  \begin{keybox}
    \textbf{Attention removes both at once.} Let every position look directly at every other
    position, in one operation. No compression through intermediate states, and no ordering
    constraint on the computation.
  \end{keybox}
\end{frame}

% =============================================================================
\section{The Attention Mechanism}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{The Attention Mechanism}{%
  A learned, weighted lookup --- which is a more useful way
  to read it than the formula suggests.}

% --- Slide: scaled dot-product -----------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Scaled Dot-Product Attention \parencite{Vaswani2017}}
  \begin{definitionbox}{The whole mechanism}
    \[
      \text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V
    \]
    $Q$ = queries, $K$ = keys, $V$ = values, $d_k$ = key dimension.
  \end{definitionbox}

  Read it as a \textbf{lookup table with soft matching}. Each position emits a \textbf{query}:
  \emph{``what am I looking for?''} Every position also offers a \textbf{key}: \emph{``here is
  what I have''} --- and a \textbf{value}: \emph{``here is what I would contribute.''}

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  $QK^\top$ scores every query against every key. The softmax turns those scores into weights
  that sum to one. The output is a weighted average of the values, with more weight where query
  and key matched.

  \textbf{Why divide by $\sqrt{d_k}$?} Dot products grow with dimension, and large inputs push
  the softmax toward one-hot, where its gradient nearly vanishes. The scaling keeps it trainable.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: what it does for forecasting -------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{What That Buys a Forecaster}
  Concretely: predicting units for the first week of December, the model can place high weight on
  \emph{last} December and on the surrounding weeks, and near-zero weight on an unremarkable
  Tuesday in March.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  Two properties make that different from anything so far.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \textbf{The weights are learned, not specified.} You do not tell the model that lag 52 matters.
  Compare that with LASSO in Lecture 6, where you built \texttt{lag52} by hand and the penalty
  decided whether to keep it.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \textbf{The path is direct.} Attention reaches week $t-52$ in \emph{one} operation, where a
  recurrent model needs 52 successive state updates and a CNN needs a receptive field that wide.
  Distance costs attention nothing.

  \begin{warningbox}
    The price is quadratic: scoring every position against every other is $O(T^2)$ in time and
    memory --- fine at $T = 26$, painful at $T = 10{,}000$.
  \end{warningbox}
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: multi-head -------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Multi-Head Attention}
  One attention operation produces one set of weights --- one notion of what is relevant. That is
  limiting, because different kinds of relevance coexist.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{Multi-head attention} runs $h$ attention operations in parallel on different learned
  projections of the same input, then concatenates:
  \[
    \text{MultiHead}(Q,K,V) = \text{concat}(\text{head}_1, \ldots, \text{head}_h)\, W^O
  \]
  \vspace{-0.3cm}

  Each head can specialize. In a forecasting model one head might track weekly position, another
  the annual cycle, another the effect of promotional flags --- all attending over the same
  window at once.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{The cost is not what you would guess.} Each head works in a lower-dimensional subspace
  of size $d_{\text{model}}/h$, so eight heads cost about the same as one head at full width.
  You buy the diversity nearly for free.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \muted{\footnotesize\itshape Our model below uses \texttt{nhead=4} with
  \texttt{d\_model=64} --- four heads of 16 dimensions each.}
\end{frame}

\section{The Transformer Encoder}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{The Transformer Encoder}{%
  Attention, plus the three pieces that make it trainable.}

% --- Slide: the block --------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{The Encoder Block}
  A Transformer encoder layer is four components in a fixed arrangement.
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{p{3.5cm}p{9.0cm}}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Multi-head attention} & Every position attends to every position. \\[3pt]
      \textbf{Residual + LayerNorm} & $\mathbf{z} = \text{LayerNorm}(\mathbf{x} + \text{MultiHead}(\mathbf{x}))$.
      The skip connection from Lecture 8, doing the same job. \\[3pt]
      \textbf{Feed-forward sublayer} & A small MLP applied to each position independently ---
      the Lecture 7 network, at every step. \\[3pt]
      \textbf{Residual + LayerNorm} & Again, around the feed-forward block. \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  Stack $N$ of those and you have the encoder. Nothing in the block is new to you except
  attention itself --- the residual connections are ResNet's, and the feed-forward sublayer is
  the network from Lecture 7.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \textbf{One thing is conspicuously missing, and it is not an oversight.} Nothing above depends
  on the \emph{order} of the input.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: positional encoding ----------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Positional Encoding: Attention Cannot Tell Time}
  Attention computes a weighted average over positions. Averaging does not care about order ---
  shuffle the input sequence and every attention output is unchanged.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{Attention is permutation-invariant.} For language that is fatal, and for forecasting it
  is worse: a model that cannot distinguish last week from thirty weeks ago is not forecasting at
  all.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  The fix is to add position information to the input itself, before attention sees it. The
  original paper uses fixed sinusoids of different frequencies:
  \[
    PE_{(t, 2i)} = \sin\!\left(\frac{t}{10000^{2i/d}}\right),
    \qquad
    PE_{(t, 2i+1)} = \cos\!\left(\frac{t}{10000^{2i/d}}\right)
  \]
  \vspace{-0.3cm}

  Each position gets a distinct pattern across dimensions, and nearby positions get similar
  patterns --- so the model can learn relative distance, not just absolute index.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \textbf{We measure what removing it costs on the next slide but one.}
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: why they took over ------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Why Transformers Took Over}
  Three properties, in the order they mattered commercially.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \textbf{Parallelism.} Every position is computed at once, so training scales across hardware in
  a way recurrence never could. This, more than accuracy, is why the architecture won --- it
  could absorb far more data and compute per unit of wall-clock time.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{Direct long-range paths.} Any position reaches any other in one step, so there is no
  distance at which information degrades.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{Scale.} The architecture kept improving as parameters and data grew, well past the
  point where earlier architectures plateaued. BERT \parencite{Devlin2019} is an encoder stack
  used for understanding; GPT is a decoder stack used for generation. Both are this block,
  repeated.

  \begin{warningbox}
    Every one of those advantages is about \textbf{large data and long sequences}. Hold that
    thought against a 26-week window and 5{,}940 training examples.
  \end{warningbox}
\end{frame}

\section{Transformers for Time Series}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Transformers for Time Series}{%
  The implementation, the measurement, and an honest reading of it.}

% --- Slide: PyTorch ----------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Transformer Encoder in PyTorch}
  \begin{examplebox}{Minimal forecasting encoder}
    {\tiny\linespread{1.15}\selectfont
    \texttt{layer = nn.TransformerEncoderLayer(d\_model=64, nhead=4,}\\
    \texttt{\ \ \ \ \ \ \ dim\_feedforward=128, dropout=0.1, batch\_first=True)}\\
    \texttt{self.enc = nn.TransformerEncoder(layer, num\_layers=2)}\\
    \texttt{self.inp = nn.Linear(n\_features, 64)}\\
    \texttt{self.fc\ \ = nn.Linear(64, 1)}\\[3pt]
    \texttt{def forward(self, x):}\\
    \texttt{\ \ h = self.inp(x) + self.pe\ \ \ \ \ \# position must be added}\\
    \texttt{\ \ return self.fc(self.enc(h)[:, -1, :]).squeeze(-1)}
    }
  \end{examplebox}
  \texttt{nn.TransformerEncoderLayer} gives you the whole block --- but not the positional
  encoding. You add that yourself, and forgetting is a silent failure.

  \muted{\footnotesize\itshape Multi-step forecasting also needs a causal mask.}
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: results ----------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Does It Work?}
  Same weekly panel, same 26-week windows, same test block as everything since Homework 3.
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{lrr}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Model} & \textbf{Test RMSE} & \textbf{Parameters} \\
      \midrule
      LASSO, 46 engineered features & \textbf{744} & 46 \\
      XGBoost & 781 & --- \\
      Vanilla RNN & \textbf{842} & 4{,}545 \\
      \textbf{Transformer encoder}, 2 layers & \textbf{990} & 67{,}329 \\
      Random forest & 899 & --- \\
      LSTM & 987 & 17{,}985 \\
      1D CNN, best variant & 1{,}202 & 8{,}161 \\
      Seasonal naive & 2{,}152 & 0 \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{-0.05cm}
  The Transformer beats the CNN and \textbf{ties the LSTM} --- 990 against 998, a difference
  smaller than the run-to-run spread. It loses to a vanilla RNN holding \textbf{one-fifteenth} as
  many parameters, and to a 46-coefficient linear model.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: why it didn't win -------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Reading That Result Honestly}
  Every advantage on the ``why Transformers took over'' slide is an advantage \emph{at scale}, and
  this problem has none of the scale.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{p{4.3cm}p{8.2cm}}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Parallel computation} & Real, but irrelevant --- the RNN trains in seconds either way. \\[3pt]
      \textbf{Direct long-range paths} & Worth little when the signal is dominated by recent weeks. \\[3pt]
      \textbf{Scales with data} & 5{,}940 training windows is not the regime where that pays. \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{-0.05cm}
  \textbf{And positional encoding is doing real work, which we can measure.} Remove it and the
  same model must forecast from an unordered bag of 26 weeks:
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{lr}
      \toprule
      Transformer \textbf{with} positional encoding & \textbf{990} \\
      Transformer \textbf{without} positional encoding & 1{,}282 \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{-0.05cm}
  \textbf{292 RMSE} --- dropping it below even the 1D CNN. That gap is the price of permutation
  invariance, and the reason the sinusoids exist.
\end{frame}

\section{Key Takeaways}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Key Takeaways}{%
  Six architectures, one series, and what the ranking actually taught us.}

% --- Slide: full comparison ---------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Sequence Architecture Comparison}
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{llll}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Architecture} & \textbf{Long range} & \textbf{Parallel} & \textbf{Needs} \\
      \midrule
      1D CNN & receptive field only & yes & modest data \\
      Vanilla RNN & $\sim$10--30 steps & no & modest data \\
      LSTM & hundreds of steps & no & more data \\
      Transformer & unlimited, $O(T^2)$ & \textbf{yes} & \textbf{much more data} \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{Choose by the structure of your problem, not by recency of the architecture.} Short
  window and recent-lag signal: a plain RNN, or honestly a linear model. Long sequences, abundant
  data, dependencies at arbitrary distance: a Transformer, and nothing else comes close.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  That second case is language, which is why this architecture reshaped that field and has been
  slower to displace gradient boosting in tabular forecasting --- where the M5 competition was won
  by ensembles of exactly the methods in Lectures 5 and 6.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: takeaways --------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Lecture 9 Part 2: Key Takeaways}
  \begin{keybox}
    {\footnotesize
    \begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
      \item \textbf{Attention} is a learned soft lookup: score every query against every key,
            softmax the scores, average the values. Distance in the sequence costs nothing.

      \item The price is \textbf{quadratic} in sequence length --- fine at 26 steps, the central
            constraint at 10{,}000.

      \item \textbf{Multi-head} attention runs several notions of relevance in parallel, at
            roughly the cost of one, by splitting the dimension.

      \item An encoder block is attention $+$ \textbf{residual} $+$ LayerNorm $+$ a feed-forward
            sublayer. Only attention is new --- the residuals are ResNet, the sublayer is
            Lecture 7.

      \item \textbf{Attention is permutation-invariant}, so position must be added explicitly.
            PyTorch does not do this for you, and omitting it fails silently.

      \item On our panel the Transformer beat the LSTM and CNN, and \textbf{lost to a vanilla
            RNN with one-fifteenth the parameters}. Its advantages are advantages at scale.
    \end{enumerate}
    }
  \end{keybox}
  \muted{\itshape Next: Bayesian Statistics I --- a different answer to the question of what a
  forecast should tell you.}
\end{frame}
