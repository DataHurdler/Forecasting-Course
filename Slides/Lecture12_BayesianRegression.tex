\section{Bayesian Linear Regression}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Bayesian Linear Regression}{%
  The same regression you already know ---
  returning a distribution for every coefficient.}

% --- Slide: OLS vs Bayesian ---------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{OLS vs.\ Bayesian Regression: What Changes?}
  You have fitted linear models since Lecture 2, and regularized them in Lecture 6. OLS solves
  for a single best coefficient vector:
  \[
    \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{y}
  \]
  \vspace{-0.25cm}

  One number per predictor, with a standard error resting on large-sample theory. The Bayesian
  version replaces that with the update rule from Lecture 10:
  \[
    p(\boldsymbol{\beta} \mid \mathbf{y}) \;\propto\; p(\mathbf{y} \mid \boldsymbol{\beta}) \cdot p(\boldsymbol{\beta})
  \]
  \vspace{-0.25cm}

  \begin{keybox}
    \textbf{Every coefficient comes back as a distribution.} Not an estimate plus an error bar
    computed from an asymptotic formula --- a full posterior you can ask questions of directly.
  \end{keybox}

  And one thing you know reappears: \textbf{regularization is a prior}. A Normal prior on $\beta$
  is Ridge; a Laplace prior is LASSO. Lecture 6's penalty is, here, a statement of belief.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: PyMC ---------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Bayesian Linear Regression in PyMC}
  \begin{examplebox}{Log units on log price, SNAP and events}
    {\tiny\linespread{1.0}\selectfont
    \texttt{with pm.Model() as model:}\\
    \texttt{\ \ a\ \ \ \ \ \ = pm.Normal("a", y.mean(), 1)}\\
    \texttt{\ \ b\_price = pm.Normal("b\_price", 0, 1)\ \ \ \ \ \# weakly informative}\\
    \texttt{\ \ b\_snap\ = pm.Normal("b\_snap",\ 0, 1)}\\
    \texttt{\ \ b\_event = pm.Normal("b\_event", 0, 1)}\\
    \texttt{\ \ sigma\ \ \ = pm.Exponential("sigma", 1.0)}\\
    \texttt{\ \ mu = a + b\_price*lpc + b\_snap*snap + b\_event*event}\\
    \texttt{\ \ pm.Normal("obs", mu=mu, sigma=sigma, observed=y)}\\
    \texttt{\ \ idata = pm.sample(1000, tune=1500, chains=4)}
    }
  \end{examplebox}
  \textbf{Do not standardize log price} --- with both in logs \texttt{b\_price} is an
  \textbf{elasticity}, percent change in demand per percent change in price. \texttt{lpc} is log
  price centered within series, which fixed ESS 184 $\rightarrow$ 3{,}097.

\end{frame}

\section{Reading the Posterior}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Reading the Posterior}{%
  Three questions you can ask a distribution
  that you cannot ask a point estimate.}

% --- Slide: reading a coefficient ------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Reading a Posterior Coefficient}
  Fitted on the weekly panel, controlling for series and trend:
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{lrrl}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Coefficient} & \textbf{Mean} & \textbf{94\% HDI} & \textbf{Reading} \\
      \midrule
      \texttt{b\_price} & $-0.493$ & $[-0.596,\ -0.387]$ & Near the elastic boundary \\
      \texttt{b\_trend} & $\phantom{-}0.110$ & $[\phantom{-}0.107,\ \phantom{-}0.113]$ & About 11\% growth per year \\
      \texttt{b\_snap} & $\phantom{-}0.036$ & $[\phantom{-}0.027,\ \phantom{-}0.046]$ & About a 3.7\% SNAP-week lift \\
      \texttt{b\_event} & $-0.003$ & $[-0.011,\ \phantom{-}0.004]$ & \textbf{Spans zero} \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{-0.05cm}
  The \textbf{HDI} --- highest density interval --- is the shortest interval containing 94\% of
  the posterior. Unlike a confidence interval it says what everyone wants it to say: there is a
  94\% probability the coefficient lies in there.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{The last row is the useful one.} \texttt{b\_event}'s interval spans zero, so the data
  do not establish a direction. That is a more honest report than ``not significant,'' because it
  also shows the effect is \emph{small} --- at most about half a percent either way.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: probability questions ---------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Asking the Posterior a Direct Question}
  The posterior is a set of samples, so any question about $\beta$ is answered by counting.

  \begin{examplebox}{One line each}
    {\scriptsize\linespread{1.05}\selectfont
    \texttt{b = idata.posterior["b\_price"].values.ravel()}\\[2pt]
    \texttt{(b < 0).mean()\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \# P(demand falls when price rises)}\\
    \texttt{(b < -0.5).mean()\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \# P(elasticity beyond -0.5)}\\
    \texttt{((b > -0.5) \& (b < 0)).mean()\ \ \# P(inelastic but negative)}
    }
  \end{examplebox}

  On our fit, $P(\text{elasticity} < 0) = \mathbf{1.00}$ --- the direction is not in doubt. But
  $P(\text{elasticity} < -0.5) = \mathbf{0.446}$: the data cannot settle whether demand is elastic
  or inelastic.

  \begin{keybox}
    \textbf{Two findings, and a p-value conflates them.} ``Significant'' reports only the
    direction. The question the business asks --- \emph{how big?} --- gets the answer 46/54.
  \end{keybox}

  \muted{\footnotesize\itshape Posterior mass inside a range of negligible values is a
  \textbf{ROPE} --- region of practical equivalence.}
\end{frame}

\section{Causal Structure and DAGs}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Causal Structure and DAGs}{%
  Which coefficients mean what you think they mean.}

% --- Slide: DAGs ----------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Directed Acyclic Graphs}
  A \textbf{DAG} is a picture of what you believe causes what: \textbf{nodes} are variables,
  \textbf{arrows} run from cause to effect, and \textbf{acyclic} means nothing causes itself.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  It is drawn before the model, from domain knowledge, and its purpose is to tell you
  \emph{which variables belong in the regression}.

  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{p{2.6cm}p{9.6cm}}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Confounder} & Causes both your predictor and your outcome. \textbf{Must} be
      controlled for, or its influence is absorbed into your coefficient. \\[3pt]
      \textbf{Collider} & Is caused by both. Controlling for it \textbf{creates} a spurious
      association that was not there. \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.05cm}
  \textbf{That second row is why ``add every control you have'' is bad advice.} Which variables
  to include is a question about causal structure, and no amount of model fit will answer it.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \muted{\footnotesize\itshape Whether a given DAG is right is a substantive question about your
  business, not a statistical one. This course uses DAGs to reason about what a coefficient
  means --- estimating causal effects credibly is a separate subject.}
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: confounding measured --------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Confounding, Measured}
  Our panel has thirty store-category series. Categories differ in \emph{both} typical price and
  typical volume --- so series identity is an arrow into price \emph{and} into units. A textbook
  confounder.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  Fit the same elasticity twice:
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{lrr}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Model} & \textbf{Elasticity} & \textbf{94\% HDI} \\
      \midrule
      Price, SNAP, events only & $-2.805$ & $[-2.856,\ -2.756]$ \\
      \ \ $+$ series effects and trend & $\mathbf{-0.493}$ & $[-0.596,\ -0.387]$ \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{-0.05cm}
  \textbf{The uncontrolled estimate is nearly six times too large} --- it claims demand collapses
  when price rises. It is not noisier either: its interval is \emph{half the width}. It is
  confidently, precisely wrong.

  \begin{warningbox}
    A tight credible interval says the model is sure. It says nothing about whether the model is
    answering your question. Bayesian machinery does not protect you from omitting a confounder
    --- only the DAG does.
  \end{warningbox}
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: scenario analysis ------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Scenario Analysis with Posterior Samples}
  Because the posterior is samples, you can push a business scenario through it and get a
  \textbf{distribution of outcomes} rather than a single projected number.

  \begin{examplebox}{What if we raise price 5\%?}
    {\scriptsize\linespread{1.05}\selectfont
    \texttt{b = idata.posterior["b\_price"].values.ravel()}\\
    \texttt{pct\_change = 100 * (np.exp(b * np.log(1.05)) - 1)}\\[2pt]
    \texttt{pct\_change.mean(), np.percentile(pct\_change, [3, 97])}
    }
  \end{examplebox}

  With an elasticity near $-0.493$, a 5\% price rise implies roughly a \textbf{2.4\% fall in
  units}, 94\% of the posterior between $-2.9\%$ and $-1.9\%$ --- so you can report the
  pessimistic end too.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \textbf{Then the decision follows.} Revenue rises if the volume loss is smaller than the price
  gain: here $+2.5\%$ on average, and $P(\text{revenue rises}) = 1.00$ across every posterior
  sample. Note this holds \emph{even though} the elasticity might be past $-0.5$.

  \muted{\footnotesize\itshape Valid only to the extent the DAG is --- this is a causal claim
  about intervening on price.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Course Method Map}
  \begin{center}

    \begin{tabular}{lllll}

      \textbf{Method} & \textbf{Data} & \textbf{Uncertainty} & \textbf{Interpretable} & \textbf{Strengths} \\
      \midrule
      ETS / ARIMA (L01--02) & Univariate TS & CI & High & Fast, automatic \\
      GAM / Prophet (L03) & TS + predictors & Limited & High & Nonlinear + interpretable \\
      Decision Tree (L04) & Tabular & No & High & Rule-based explanation \\
      Random Forest (L05) & Tabular & OOB CI & Medium & Robust, minimal tuning \\
      XGBoost (L06) & Tabular & No & Low & Best tabular accuracy \\
      FFN / 1D CNN (L07--08) & Tabular / TS & No & Low & Smooth functions, fast \\
      LSTM / Transformer (L09) & Sequences & No & Very low & Long-range dependencies \\
      Bayesian (L10--12) & Any & Full posterior & High & Uncertainty, small samples \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}
  \begin{keybox}
    \small No single method dominates. Choose based on:
    (1) data type and size, (2) need for uncertainty quantification,
    (3) need for interpretability, (4) time to deploy.
    In practice, combine: use XGBoost for the point forecast
    and a Bayesian model for uncertainty intervals.
  \end{keybox}
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: Key takeaways ---------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Lecture 12: Key Takeaways}
  \begin{keybox}
    {\small
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item \textbf{Bayesian linear regression} places priors on coefficients
            and returns a full posterior distribution for each $\beta_j$.
            A Normal prior is equivalent to Ridge regularization;
            a Laplace prior is equivalent to LASSO.

      \item Posterior coefficient plots give:
            posterior mean, HDI, and $P(\beta_j > 0)$ ---
            richer information than a single $p$-value.

      \item \textbf{DAGs} make the causal structure explicit,
            helping identify confounders (must include) and
            colliders (must exclude). Always draw the DAG before
            specifying a regression model.

      \item \textbf{Scenario analysis} with posterior samples:
            plug any counterfactual $\mathbf{x}^*$ into posterior draws
            to get a full predictive distribution for that scenario.

      \item \textbf{No single method is best for all forecasting problems.}
            Build intuition for when to use each tool in this course's toolkit,
            and combine methods (e.g., point forecast + Bayesian uncertainty)
            when the decision stakes are high.
    \end{enumerate}
    }
  \end{keybox}
\end{frame}
