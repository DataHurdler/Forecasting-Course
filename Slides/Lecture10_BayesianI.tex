\section{Why Bayesian Thinking?}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Why Bayesian Thinking?}{%
  A different answer to the question of what a forecast should tell you.}

% --- Slide: frequentist vs bayesian ------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Frequentist vs.\ Bayesian: What Is Random?}
  The whole difference is which thing you treat as uncertain.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{p{2.2cm}p{4.9cm}p{5.0cm}}
      \toprule
       & \textbf{Frequentist} & \textbf{Bayesian} \\
      \midrule
      The parameter & A fixed, unknown constant & A quantity you are uncertain about \\[3pt]
      The data & Random --- one draw of many possible samples & Fixed --- it is what you observed \\[3pt]
      The answer & A point estimate and a standard error & A full distribution over values \\[3pt]
      Prior belief & No formal place for it & Stated explicitly, and updated \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  Everything else follows from that first row. If the parameter is fixed, you can only describe
  how your \emph{estimate} would vary across hypothetical repeated samples. If the parameter is
  uncertain, you can describe your uncertainty about \emph{it} directly --- which is almost
  always the question someone actually asked.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: what a posterior buys --------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{What This Buys You in a Meeting}
  A 95\% confidence interval of $[0.02,\ 0.08]$ does \emph{not} mean there is a 95\% chance the
  parameter lies in that range. It means that intervals built this way would contain the true
  value 95\% of the time across repeated samples. That is a statement about the procedure.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  A 95\% \textbf{credible} interval of $[0.02,\ 0.08]$ does mean there is a 95\% probability the
  parameter lies in that range, given your model and data.

  \begin{keybox}
    That is not a technicality. It is the difference between a sentence you can say out loud to a
    decision-maker and one you have to keep correcting.
  \end{keybox}

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  And because the answer is a full distribution, you can ask questions of it directly:
  \emph{What is the probability the promotion lifts demand by more than 5\%? What is the chance
  we stock out if we order 400 units?} Those are the questions a planner has, and a point
  estimate with a standard error does not answer them.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \muted{\footnotesize\itshape The cost is that you must state a prior, and defend it. That is
  the rest of this lecture.}
\end{frame}

\section{Bayes' Theorem}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Bayes' Theorem}{%
  One equation, and it is a bookkeeping rule rather than a philosophy.}

% --- Slide: Bayes' theorem ---------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Bayes' Theorem \parencite{Gelman2013}}
  \begin{definitionbox}{The update rule}
    \[
      \underbrace{p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})}_{\text{posterior}}
      = \frac{\overbrace{p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)}^{\text{likelihood}}
              \cdot \overbrace{p(\theta)}^{\text{prior}}}
             {\underbrace{p(\mathcal{D})}_{\text{evidence}}}
    \]
  \end{definitionbox}

  Read it as a sentence. \textbf{What I believe after seeing the data} equals \textbf{how well
  each value explains the data}, times \textbf{what I believed beforehand}, rescaled to sum to one.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  The denominator is only that rescaling --- it does not change the \emph{shape}. So the working
  form is:
  \[
    \text{posterior} \;\propto\; \text{likelihood} \times \text{prior}
  \]
  \vspace{-0.3cm}

  \textbf{You will not compute this by hand}, and this course never asks you to. You are
  responsible for choosing the prior, checking the sampler worked, and reading the posterior.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: the update, graphically ------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{The Update, in Pictures}
  Take a concrete case: estimating a conversion rate $\theta$ from 200 trials.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{p{2.6cm}p{9.9cm}}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Prior} & A curve over $\theta$ from 0 to 1, showing what you thought before. Broad
      if you know little; peaked if you have history. \\[3pt]
      \textbf{Likelihood} & A second curve, from the data alone: 30 successes out of 200 peaks
      near 0.15. \\[3pt]
      \textbf{Posterior} & Multiply the two curves point by point and rescale. It sits
      \emph{between} them, closer to whichever was more confident. \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \textbf{Our numbers:} the prior is centred on 0.10, the data say 0.15, and the posterior mean
  lands at \textbf{0.14} --- between them, nearer the data, because 200 observations outweigh a
  prior worth about 50.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  More data sharpens the likelihood, so the posterior moves toward it and the prior matters less.
  With little data the prior does most of the work --- a feature when it is defensible, a problem
  when it is not.
\end{frame}

\section{Prior Distributions}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Prior Distributions}{%
  The part you have to justify, and the part people worry about.}

% --- Slide: common priors ----------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Priors You Will Actually Use}
  A prior is a distribution over a parameter, chosen to match what that parameter \emph{can} be.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{p{2.9cm}p{3.0cm}p{6.4cm}}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Prior} & \textbf{Support} & \textbf{Use it for} \\
      \midrule
      $\theta \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ & $[0, 1]$ & Rates and proportions --- conversion,
      click-through, defect rate \\[3pt]
      $\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2)$ & all reals & Regression coefficients, trends,
      anything that can be negative \\[3pt]
      $\theta \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda)$ & $[0, \infty)$ & Standard deviations, waiting times,
      anything that must be positive \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \textbf{The support is the first thing to get right.} Putting a Normal prior on a standard
  deviation allows negative values, which are impossible --- the sampler will struggle and the
  model may not be wrong so much as incoherent.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \muted{\footnotesize\itshape $\text{Beta}(5, 45)$ has mean $5/50 = 0.1$ --- a prior centred on
  a 10\% rate, worth about 50 observations of prior evidence.}
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: choosing a defensible prior --------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Choosing a Prior You Can Defend}
  The standard objection to Bayesian work is that the prior is arbitrary. The standard answer is
  that you should not be choosing arbitrary priors.

  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{p{3.3cm}p{9.0cm}}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Weakly informative} & Rules out the absurd, stays agnostic about the plausible. A
      Normal(0, 1) on a standardized coefficient says ``probably not larger than about 2,''
      which is nearly always true and nearly never controversial. \textbf{This is the default.} \\[3pt]
      \textbf{Informative} & Encodes real prior evidence --- last year's conversion rate, a
      published elasticity. Defensible when you can name the source. \\[3pt]
      \textbf{Flat / ``uninformative''} & Sounds neutral, often is not, and can be improper.
      Avoid it as a reflex choice. \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \textbf{The honest framing:} a prior is an assumption stated in public, where it can be argued
  with. Frequentist analyses contain assumptions too --- functional form, variable selection,
  sampling scheme --- they are just not written as distributions.
\end{frame}

\section{Posterior Inference with PyMC}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Posterior Inference with PyMC}{%
  Sampling, the code, and how to tell whether the answer is trustworthy.}

% --- Slide: MCMC -------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{MCMC: The Big Idea}
  For any model more complicated than a textbook example, the rescaling denominator in Bayes'
  theorem cannot be computed. So we do not compute the posterior --- we \textbf{draw samples
  from it}, and work with the samples.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{The picture to hold.} You are exploring a hilly landscape in the dark, where height is
  posterior probability. You take a step, and if the ground is higher you move; if it is lower
  you usually move back. Over thousands of steps you spend most of your time on the high ground.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  The record of where you walked \emph{is} the answer. A histogram of your visited positions has
  the shape of the posterior, without ever having computed it.

  \begin{keybox}
    \textbf{The samples are the answer.} Posterior mean, credible interval, and the probability
    of any event you care about are all just summaries of that list of visited values ---
    counted, not derived.
  \end{keybox}

  \muted{\footnotesize\itshape PyMC's default sampler is NUTS, which takes smarter steps than
  the random walk above. The intuition is unchanged.}
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: PyMC first model -------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{PyMC: A First Model}
  \begin{examplebox}{Estimating a conversion rate --- 30 successes in 200 trials}
    {\tiny\linespread{1.2}\selectfont
    \texttt{import pymc as pm}\\[2pt]
    \texttt{with pm.Model() as model:}\\
    \texttt{\ \ \ \ theta = pm.Beta("theta", alpha=5, beta=45)\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \# prior}\\
    \texttt{\ \ \ \ obs\ \ = pm.Binomial("obs", n=200, p=theta,\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \# likelihood}\\
    \texttt{\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ observed=30)}\\
    \texttt{\ \ \ \ idata = pm.sample(2000, tune=1000, chains=4)\ \ \ \ \ \# posterior}\\[3pt]
    \texttt{import arviz as az}\\
    \texttt{az.plot\_posterior(idata)}
    }
  \end{examplebox}
  A PyMC model describes \textbf{how the data could have been generated}: what I believe about
  the rate, and how observations arise given it. You write the story; the sampler infers.

  \texttt{tune=1000} discards the first 1{,}000 steps, while the sampler is still finding the
  high ground. \texttt{chains=4} runs four independent walks --- which is what makes the next
  slide possible.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: diagnostics ------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Did the Sampler Actually Work? \parencite{MartinKumarLao2021}}
  MCMC can fail quietly. It always returns samples; whether they came from the posterior is a
  separate question.
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{p{2.5cm}p{3.0cm}p{6.6cm}}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Check} & \textbf{Want} & \textbf{What it means} \\
      \midrule
      $\hat{R}$ & $< 1.01$ & Did the chains land in the same place? If not, nothing else
      matters. \\[2pt]
      ESS & $> 400$ & Effective sample size --- 8{,}000 correlated draws may carry as much
      information as 200 independent ones. \\[2pt]
      Divergences & $0$ & The sampler hit terrain it could not navigate. Even a few means the
      posterior is explored incorrectly. \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \texttt{az.summary(idata)} prints $\hat{R}$ and ESS per parameter. On the model above:
  $\hat{R} = 1.00$, ESS 3{,}275, zero divergences. \textbf{Report all three} --- skipping this is
  the same class of error as \texttt{KFold} on a time series.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: prior predictive checks -----------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Prior Predictive Checks \parencite{McElreath2020}}
  Before touching the data, simulate from the prior alone. If it implies nonsense, you want to
  know now.

  \begin{examplebox}{Two lines}
    {\scriptsize\linespread{1.0}\selectfont
    \texttt{with model:}\\
    \texttt{\ \ \ \ prior\_pred = pm.sample\_prior\_predictive(500)}\\[2pt]
    \texttt{az.plot\_ppc(prior\_pred, group="prior")}
    }
  \end{examplebox}

  \textbf{Ask whether the simulated data could plausibly have come from your business.}
  Impossible values --- negative sales, probabilities above one --- mean the prior is
  misspecified, not merely wide. Data spanning a billion units means it is too vague; identical
  simulations mean it is too tight.

  \begin{keybox}
    A prior predictive check is \textbf{model debugging} that costs two lines --- and the habit
    most often skipped.
  \end{keybox}
\end{frame}

\section{Key Takeaways}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Key Takeaways}{%
  Bayesian inference: beliefs in, beliefs out.
  Data updates your prior into a posterior.}

% --- Slide: Key takeaways ---------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Lecture 10: Key Takeaways}
  \begin{keybox}
    {\small
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item \textbf{Bayesian inference} treats parameters as random variables
            with probability distributions. The posterior distribution
            $p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})$ represents updated beliefs after seeing data.

      \item \textbf{Bayes' theorem:} posterior $\propto$ likelihood $\times$ prior.
            The normalizing constant $p(\mathcal{D})$ is usually intractable,
            so we sample instead of computing it.

      \item \textbf{Priors} encode prior knowledge. Use weakly informative priors
            (Beta, Normal, HalfNormal) to constrain parameters to plausible values
            without overwhelming the data.

      \item \textbf{MCMC} (specifically NUTS in PyMC) draws samples from the posterior.
            From samples, compute any summary statistic: mean, median, HDI, or
            the probability that a parameter exceeds a threshold.

      \item \textbf{Prior predictive checks} verify that your model generates plausible
            data before fitting. Run them every time.
    \end{enumerate}
    }
  \end{keybox}
  \muted{\itshape Next: Bayesian Statistics II ---
  Bayesian time series models, trend and seasonality, hierarchical models.}
\end{frame}
