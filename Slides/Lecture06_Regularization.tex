\section{The Problem: Too Many Predictors}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{The Problem: Too Many Predictors}{%
  Feature engineering gives us more predictors than we can safely estimate.
  Something has to decide which ones survive.}

% --- Slide: Why regularize ---------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Why Regularize?}
  OLS minimizes $\sum_{i}(y_i - \mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta})^2$. A weekly model with lags,
  calendar dummies, promotions, and macro series can easily carry $k = 60$ predictors on
  $n = 150$ observations --- OLS will fit that sample beautifully and forecast badly.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  Three things go wrong at once. The variance of $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ grows with $k$.
  Correlated predictors produce unstable, wildly large coefficients with opposing signs. And
  once $k > n$ there is no unique solution at all.
  \textbf{Regularization} adds a penalty on coefficient size:
  \[
    \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_i (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}_{\text{fit}}
    + \underbrace{\lambda \cdot \text{Penalty}(\boldsymbol{\beta})}_{\text{complexity}}
  \]
  \vspace{-0.2cm}
  You accept a little bias to buy a large reduction in variance --- and for a forecast, that
  trade is almost always worth making.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: Subset selection -------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Subset Selection: The Classical Answer}
  Before shrinkage, the standard approach was to \emph{count} predictors rather than shrink
  them \parencite{Guyon2003}.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{Best subset} fits all $2^k$ models and picks the winner --- exact, and
  computationally hopeless past $k \approx 30$. \textbf{Stepwise} adds the strongest predictor
  or drops the weakest, one at a time; it is greedy, and can miss the best combination
  entirely. Either way the choice is made by adjusted $R^2$, AIC, AICc, BIC, or CV.
  \begin{warningbox}
    Subset selection is \textbf{discrete} --- a predictor is in or out. Small changes in the
    data flip predictors in and out, so the \emph{selected model} is itself high-variance.
    You have not removed the instability; you have moved it somewhere harder to see.
  \end{warningbox}

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \muted{\footnotesize This is the approach in \emph{FPP} \S7.5. Shrinkage replaces the
  in-or-out decision with a continuous dial --- more stable, and easier to tune.}
\end{frame}

\section{Shrinkage Methods}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Shrinkage Methods}{%
  Keep every predictor, but pull its coefficient toward zero.}

% --- Slide: Ridge ------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Ridge Regression ($\ell_2$)}
  \begin{definitionbox}{Ridge \parencite{HoerlKennard1970}}
    \[
      \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\text{ridge}}
      = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \;
        \sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta})^2
        \; + \; \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{k} \beta_j^2
    \]
  \end{definitionbox}
  Ridge shrinks every coefficient toward zero but sets none exactly to zero. At $\lambda = 0$
  you have OLS; as $\lambda \to \infty$ all $\beta_j \to 0$. Its particular strength is
  correlated predictors: rather than letting two collinear variables fight for the same
  coefficient, it splits the effect between them.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{Use it when} you believe most predictors carry a little signal and you want to keep
  them all.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{Standardize first.} The penalty is not scale-invariant --- a predictor measured in
  dollars is penalized differently from the same predictor in thousands of dollars. Get this
  wrong and the units decide which variables survive.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: LASSO ------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{LASSO ($\ell_1$)}
  \begin{definitionbox}{LASSO \parencite{Tibshirani1996}}
    \[
      \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\text{lasso}}
      = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \;
        \sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta})^2
        \; + \; \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{k} |\beta_j|
    \]
  \end{definitionbox}
  Swap the squared penalty for an absolute one and the behavior changes qualitatively. LASSO
  sets some coefficients \textbf{exactly} to zero, performing selection and estimation in a
  single step, and leaving a sparse model you can actually read aloud to a stakeholder.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{Use it when} you believe only a handful of predictors genuinely matter and you want
  the model to tell you which.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{But be careful what you conclude.} Among a group of highly correlated predictors,
  LASSO keeps \emph{one} more or less arbitrarily and zeros the rest. The survivor is not
  ``the important one'' --- it is the one that happened to win a coin toss between
  near-identical variables.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: Why LASSO zeros out ----------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Why LASSO Zeros Out and Ridge Does Not}
  Both solve ``minimize squared error subject to a budget on coefficient size.'' The
  \emph{shape} of the budget is the whole difference.
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \textbf{Ridge:} $\sum_j \beta_j^2 \le t$ --- a \textbf{circle}
      \begin{itemize}\small\itemsep0pt
        \item Smooth boundary, no corners
        \item Error contours almost never touch it on an axis
        \item So coefficients get small, never zero
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \textbf{LASSO:} $\sum_j |\beta_j| \le t$ --- a \textbf{diamond}
      \begin{itemize}\small\itemsep0pt
        \item Corners sit exactly on the axes
        \item Contours are likely to hit a corner first
        \item A corner means $\beta_j = 0$ exactly
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vspace{0.2cm}
  So the sparsity of LASSO is a geometric accident of the $\ell_1$ ball having corners --- not
  a statistical test of significance. A zeroed coefficient means ``not worth its budget
  here,'' not ``proven irrelevant.'' See \textcite{James2021} \S6.2.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: Elastic Net ------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Elastic Net: Both Penalties at Once}
  \begin{definitionbox}{Elastic Net \parencite{ZouHastie2005}}
    \[
      \sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta})^2
      \; + \; \lambda \left[ \alpha \sum_j |\beta_j|
      + (1-\alpha) \sum_j \beta_j^2 \right]
    \]
    $\lambda$ sets the total penalty; $\alpha \in [0,1]$ sets the mix ($\alpha=1$ is LASSO,
    $\alpha=0$ is Ridge).
  \end{definitionbox}
  The mix keeps LASSO's ability to zero out predictors while adding Ridge's \textbf{grouping
  effect}: correlated predictors now enter or leave together instead of one surviving
  arbitrarily.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{This is usually the right default for forecasting.} Lagged predictors are correlated
  almost by construction --- $y_{t-1}$ and $y_{t-2}$ move together --- which is precisely the
  situation where pure LASSO picks a winner at random and pure Ridge refuses to simplify.
\end{frame}

\section{Choosing the Penalty}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Choosing the Penalty}{%
  $\lambda$ is not estimated from the model. It is tuned --- and how you
  tune it decides whether the model generalizes.}

% --- Slide: Regularization path ----------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{The Regularization Path}
  Fit the model across a grid of $\lambda$ values and trace each coefficient as it moves.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  Read the path left to right. At small $\lambda$ the coefficients sit near their OLS values
  and the model is dense and unstable. As $\lambda$ grows they shrink, and under LASSO they
  drop to zero one at a time. At large $\lambda$ only the most robust predictors survive, and
  in the limit you are left with the intercept --- the mean forecast.
  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{The order in which predictors leave is the useful output.} The last survivors are
  the ones that hold up under the most pressure. Report that ordering rather than a single
  model's coefficient table --- far more stable across resamples, and far more honest about
  what the data can support.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: Choosing lambda --------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Choosing $\lambda$ by Cross-Validation}
  $\lambda$ is not estimated from the model --- it is tuned, and how you tune it decides
  whether the model generalizes.
  \begin{enumerate}\small\itemsep0pt
    \item Define a grid of $\lambda$, usually log-spaced
    \item For each $\lambda$, run walk-forward CV and record the mean validation RMSE
    \item Pick the $\lambda$ minimizing CV error, then refit on all training data
  \end{enumerate}

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \textbf{The one-standard-error rule} is worth knowing: rather than the outright minimum,
  take the \emph{largest} $\lambda$ whose CV error is within one standard error of it. You
  give up almost no accuracy and get a simpler, more stable model.
  \begin{warningbox}
    \textbf{Never use \texttt{KFold} here.} Random folds put future observations in the
    training set and past ones in validation. The CV error will look excellent and the
    forecast will fail \parencite{Bergmeir2018}. Use \texttt{TimeSeriesSplit}, exactly as in
    Lecture 1's walk-forward evaluation.
  \end{warningbox}
\end{frame}

\section{In Practice}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{In Practice}{%
  Standardize, tune on a time-aware split, and read the path.}

% --- Slide: Python -----------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Regularization in Python}
  \begin{examplebox}{Code Template}
    {\scriptsize
    \texttt{from sklearn.linear\_model import ElasticNetCV}\\
    \texttt{from sklearn.pipeline import make\_pipeline}\\
    \texttt{from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler}\\
    \texttt{from sklearn.model\_selection import TimeSeriesSplit}\\[2pt]
    \texttt{model = make\_pipeline(StandardScaler(),}\\
    \texttt{\ \ ElasticNetCV(l1\_ratio=[.1,.5,.7,.9,.95,1],}\\
    \texttt{\ \ \ cv=TimeSeriesSplit(5), random\_state=42)).fit(X\_tr, y\_tr)}
    }
  \end{examplebox}
  \texttt{make\_pipeline} is the part that matters: it keeps standardization \emph{inside}
  each CV fold. Scale on the full sample and you have leaked the validation set into
  training, and your CV error is optimistic.

  \texttt{l1\_ratio} is $\alpha$, the Ridge/LASSO mix; the \texttt{...CV} variants tune
  $\lambda$ for you.

  \muted{\footnotesize \texttt{sklearn} calls the penalty \texttt{alpha}, not
  \texttt{lambda}.}
\end{frame}

\section{Key Takeaways and Roadmap}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Key Takeaways and Roadmap}{%
  Shrinkage is the linear-model counterpart to what trees do by pruning.}

% --- Slide: Which method when -------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Linear Methods: Which One When}
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{llll}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Method} & \textbf{Penalty} & \textbf{Zeros out?} & \textbf{Best when} \\
      \midrule
      OLS & none & no & $k \ll n$, predictors uncorrelated \\
      Stepwise & discrete & yes & Few candidate predictors, need a story \\
      Ridge & $\ell_2$ & no & Many predictors, all mildly useful \\
      LASSO & $\ell_1$ & yes & Few predictors truly matter \\
      Elastic Net & both & yes & Many \emph{correlated} predictors \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}
  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{Connection to Lecture 5.} Trees and shrinkage solve the same problem in different
  geometries. XGBoost's \texttt{reg\_lambda} is literally a Ridge penalty on leaf weights,
  and its \texttt{gamma} penalizes leaf count the way LASSO penalizes nonzero coefficients.
  Regularization is not a linear-model topic --- it is how every flexible model is kept
  honest.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: Key takeaways ---------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Lecture 6: Key Takeaways}
  \begin{keybox}
    {\footnotesize
    \begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
      \item With many predictors OLS overfits: coefficient variance grows with
            $k$, and with $k > n$ there is no unique solution at all.

      \item \textbf{Subset selection} counts predictors in or out. It is discrete,
            greedy, and unstable --- small data changes flip the selected model.

      \item \textbf{Shrinkage} replaces that switch with a dial. Ridge ($\ell_2$)
            shrinks everything smoothly; LASSO ($\ell_1$) shrinks and zeros out;
            Elastic Net does both and keeps correlated predictors together.

      \item \textbf{Standardize first}, inside each CV fold --- none of these
            penalties are scale-invariant. \textbf{$\lambda$ is tuned, not
            estimated}: use \texttt{TimeSeriesSplit}, never random $K$-fold,
            which leaks the future.

      \item The \textbf{regularization path} --- the order in which predictors
            drop out --- is a more stable finding than any single coefficient table.
    \end{enumerate}
    }
  \end{keybox}
  \muted{\itshape Next: Introduction to Neural Networks (PyTorch) ---
  where these same penalties reappear as weight decay and dropout.}
\end{frame}
