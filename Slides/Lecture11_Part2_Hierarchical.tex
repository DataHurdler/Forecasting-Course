\section{Which Series May Be Pooled}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Which Series May Be Pooled}{%
  Before you pool anything, ask what the groups have in common.}

% --- Slide: the problem -----------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{The Question}
  A concrete one, and you have met it before. \textbf{Does demand rise in weeks when SNAP
  benefits are disbursed, and by how much --- store by store?} Homework 3's memo had you stock up
  for SNAP weeks store by store, which assumed those differences are real. Today we check.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{But one question comes before the model.} SNAP is a \emph{food} assistance programme.
  Measure the raw SNAP-week effect on log units in all thirty series, grouped by category:

  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{lrr}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Category} & \textbf{Mean effect} & \textbf{Spread across stores} \\
      \midrule
      \textbf{FOODS} (10 series) & $+0.089$ & 0.058 \\
      HOBBIES (10 series) & $+0.006$ & 0.010 \\
      HOUSEHOLD (10 series) & $+0.011$ & 0.014 \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.05cm}
  \textbf{These are not thirty versions of the same thing.} FOODS responds; the other two barely
  move. Pooling all thirty would tell the model they are interchangeable.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: exchangeability -------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Exchangeability: the Assumption Under Every Hierarchy}
  Partial pooling rests on one assumption, worth naming before we use it.

  \begin{definitionbox}[Exchangeable]
    Groups are \textbf{exchangeable} when, before seeing the data, you have no reason to expect
    one to differ from another in a particular direction. Swapping their labels would not change
    what you believe.
  \end{definitionbox}

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  The ten FOODS series qualify: same category, different stores, and you could not say in advance
  which store's SNAP effect should be largest. FOODS against HOBBIES does \textbf{not} --- you
  know before looking that a food-assistance programme moves food.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{The setup for today.} We work with the \textbf{ten FOODS series}, and deliberately thin
  \textbf{five} of them down to \textbf{8 weeks}. Five series are data-rich, five are data-poor.
  That imbalance is the whole point.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \muted{\footnotesize\itshape The lab pools all thirty anyway, and measures what it costs.}
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: two bad options -------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Complete Pooling and No Pooling}
  Within that exchangeable set there are two obvious approaches, and each fails in a different
  direction.
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{p{3.0cm}p{4.4cm}p{4.9cm}}
      \toprule
      & \textbf{What it does} & \textbf{How it fails} \\
      \midrule
      \textbf{Complete pooling} & One SNAP effect for all ten series. \newline
      \texttt{b = pm.Normal("b", 0, 1)} & Denies that stores differ at all. A genuinely
      different store is averaged away. \\[3pt]
      \textbf{No pooling} & A separate effect per series, estimated independently. \newline
      \texttt{shape=10} & A series with 8 weeks gets an estimate built from 8 weeks. Mostly
      noise, reported as a finding. \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.05cm}
  \textbf{You have used both already without naming them.} Homework 3's random forest pooled all
  thirty series into one model with store dummies --- close to complete pooling. Homework 1
  fitted ARIMA to one series at a time --- no pooling. The choice has always been made by
  convenience; partial pooling makes it a modelling decision and lets the data settle it.
\end{frame}

% =============================================================================
\section{Partial Pooling}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Partial Pooling}{%
  Let each series have its own effect --- drawn from a shared distribution
  the data also estimates.}

% --- Slide: the hierarchy ---------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{The Hierarchy Is One Extra Line}
  Give each series its own effect $b_j$, but say where those effects come from:
  \[
    b_j \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_b,\ \sigma_b), \qquad j = 1, \ldots, 10
  \]
  \vspace{-0.25cm}

  $\mu_b$ is the typical SNAP effect across FOODS, and $\sigma_b$ is \textbf{how much stores
  genuinely differ}. Crucially, both are estimated from the data rather than assumed.

  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{p{3.1cm}p{9.2cm}}
      \toprule
      $\sigma_b \rightarrow 0$ & The model concludes stores do not differ, and reproduces
      complete pooling. \\[3pt]
      $\sigma_b$ large & The model concludes they differ a lot, and approaches no pooling. \\[3pt]
      In between & Each $b_j$ is pulled toward $\mu_b$ --- \emph{how far} depending on how much
      data that series has. \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.05cm}
  \textbf{That last row is the whole idea.} A series with 277 weeks barely moves; one with 8 weeks
  moves a long way. Nobody chose those weights --- they fall out of the model.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: shrinkage intuition ---------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Why This Is the Right Answer, Not a Compromise}
  Partial pooling sounds like splitting the difference between two bad options. It is better than
  that, and the reason is worth stating.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  When a series has 8 observations, its unpooled estimate is mostly sampling noise. The
  hierarchy's pull toward $\mu_b$ is not a fudge --- it is the correct response to a noisy
  measurement. You already believe this in another form: it is why you would not conclude a coin
  is biased after three flips.

  \begin{keybox}
    \textbf{This is regularization again, for the sixth time.} A hierarchical prior shrinks
    per-group estimates toward a common value exactly as Ridge shrinks coefficients toward zero
    --- and here the shrinkage strength $\sigma_b$ is \emph{estimated} rather than tuned by
    cross-validation.
  \end{keybox}

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \muted{\footnotesize\itshape GAM smoothing penalty (L3) $\rightarrow$ tree pruning (L4)
  $\rightarrow$ \texttt{reg\_lambda} (L5) $\rightarrow$ Ridge and LASSO (L6) $\rightarrow$ weight
  decay (L7) $\rightarrow$ hierarchical priors (today).}
\end{frame}

\section{Fitting It, and the Trap
}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Fitting It, and the Trap}{%
  The obvious code does not work, and the reason
  is geometry rather than statistics.}

% --- Slide: PyMC ------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{The Model in PyMC}
  \begin{examplebox}{Non-centred hierarchical model}
    {\tiny\linespread{1.05}\selectfont
    \texttt{with pm.Model() as model:}\\
    \texttt{\ \ mu\_b = pm.Normal("mu\_b", 0, 0.5)\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \# typical SNAP effect}\\
    \texttt{\ \ sd\_b = pm.HalfNormal("sd\_b", 0.25)\ \ \ \ \ \# how much stores differ}\\
    \texttt{\ \ z\ \ \ \ = pm.Normal("z", 0, 1, shape=J)\ \ \ \ \# non-centred}\\
    \texttt{\ \ b\ \ \ \ = pm.Deterministic("b", mu\_b + z * sd\_b)}\\[2pt]
    \texttt{\ \ a = pm.Normal("a", y.mean(), 1, shape=J)\ \ \# per-series level}\\
    \texttt{\ \ s = pm.Exponential("s", 1.0)}\\[2pt]
    \texttt{\ \ pm.Normal("obs", mu=a[idx] + b[idx]*snap, sigma=s, observed=y)}
    }
  \end{examplebox}
  \texttt{idx} maps each row to its series, so \texttt{b[idx]} picks out the right effect for
  every observation. That indexing pattern is how every hierarchical model in PyMC is written.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{Note what is \emph{not} here.} No decision about how much to pool, no weighting scheme,
  no threshold for ``enough data.'' Those all come out of estimating $\sigma_b$.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: the funnel ------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{The Trap: Write It the Obvious Way and It Breaks}
  The natural way to write the hierarchy is \texttt{b = pm.Normal("b", mu\_b, sd\_b, shape=J)}
  --- the \emph{centred} form, which reads exactly like the equation. On our data it produces:
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{lrrl}
      \toprule
      & \textbf{Centred} & \textbf{Non-centred} & \textbf{Want} \\
      \midrule
      Divergences & \textbf{127} & \textbf{1} & 0 \\
      $\hat{R}$ & \textbf{1.044} & 1.002 & $< 1.01$ \\
      ESS (bulk) & 413 & 1{,}728 & $> 400$ \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{-0.05cm}
  \textbf{Why:} when $\sigma_b$ is small, the $b_j$ must crowd into a narrow band, and the
  posterior takes on a funnel shape --- wide where $\sigma_b$ is large, pinched where it is
  small. A sampler tuned for the wide part cannot navigate the neck, and reports divergences.

  \vspace{0.05cm}
  \textbf{The alarms do not fail alike.} ESS passes at 413; $\hat{R}$ fails at 1.044, the sort of
  near-miss people talk themselves out of. \textbf{127 divergences against a threshold of zero
  admits no such conversation.}

  \vspace{0.02cm}
  \muted{\footnotesize\itshape BMCP §4.6.1, \emph{Posterior Geometry Matters} --- the single most
  common failure in applied hierarchical modelling.}
\end{frame}

\section{Reading the Result}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Reading the Result}{%
  Shrinkage, measured --- and checked against
  data the model never saw.}

% --- Slide: the measurement -------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Shrinkage, Measured}
  Fit both models. Compare each series' estimated SNAP effect before and after pooling.
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{lrrr}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Series group} & \textbf{Unpooled spread} & \textbf{After pooling} & \textbf{Mean move} \\
      \midrule
      \textbf{Thin} --- 8 weeks (5 series) & 0.108 & 0.010 & \textbf{0.120} \\
      \textbf{Full} --- 277 weeks (5 series) & 0.055 & 0.037 & 0.015 \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{-0.05cm}
  \textbf{The data-poor series moved 8.2 times further toward the group mean than the data-rich
  ones.} Nobody told the model which series were thin. It inferred that from how much each series
  had to say.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  Read the two right-hand columns together. The thin series' apparent spread of $0.108$ nearly
  vanishes, while the full series \textbf{keep most of theirs} ($0.055 \rightarrow 0.037$). The
  hierarchy did not flatten everything: it discarded spread that 8 weeks could not support and
  kept spread that 277 weeks could.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \textbf{That is the claim to be sceptical of, so the next slide tests it.}
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: held-out check --------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Checked Against Data the Model Never Saw}
  We thinned those five series ourselves, so the weeks we removed are still there. Compare each
  estimate against the SNAP effect actually observed in the held-out weeks.

  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{lrrr}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Thin series} & \textbf{Unpooled} & \textbf{Hierarchical} & \textbf{Held-out truth} \\
      \midrule
      CA\_1\_FOODS & 0.286 & 0.097 & 0.087 \\
      CA\_3\_FOODS & 0.105 & 0.082 & 0.102 \\
      TX\_1\_FOODS & 0.052 & 0.075 & 0.082 \\
      TX\_3\_FOODS & 0.219 & 0.091 & 0.072 \\
      WI\_2\_FOODS & 0.338 & 0.103 & 0.207 \\
      \midrule
      \textbf{RMSE against truth} & \textbf{0.126} & \textbf{0.049} & --- \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{-0.05cm}
  \textbf{Partial pooling was two and a half times more accurate}, beating the unpooled fit on
  four series out of five. Those estimates were not just noisy --- they were confidently wrong,
  and the confidence came from 8 weeks of data.

  \vspace{0.05cm}
  \muted{\footnotesize\itshape Borrowing strength is not a figure of speech: the thin series were
  estimated better \emph{because} the other five existed.}
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: the business answer ---------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{What the Model Actually Concluded}
  The estimated hierarchy: $\mu_b = 0.077$ with a 94\% HDI of $[0.016,\ 0.150]$, and
  $\sigma_b = 0.066$.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  In words: a SNAP week lifts FOODS units by roughly \textbf{8\%}, and stores genuinely differ
  around that by about \textbf{7 percentage points}. Both numbers are answers, and the second one
  is the one no point estimate would have given you.

  \begin{warningbox}
    \textbf{It does not license the store-level policy the unpooled fit suggested.} An analyst
    reading the unpooled numbers would report uplifts from $5\%$ to $40\%$ and stock accordingly.
    The held-out weeks say that range was mostly noise --- but $\sigma_b = 0.066$ says it is not
    \emph{all} noise either.
  \end{warningbox}

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \textbf{The defensible recommendation:} plan on an 8\% FOODS uplift everywhere, allow real but
  modest store variation around it, and do not act on a store's own estimate until that store has
  the weeks to support one.
\end{frame}

\section{Key Takeaways}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Key Takeaways}{%
  What hierarchy buys, and what it costs.}

\begin{frame}{Lecture 11 Part 2: Key Takeaways}
  \begin{keybox}
    {\footnotesize
    \begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
      \item \textbf{Ask what may be pooled before you pool it.} Groups must be
            \textbf{exchangeable}. FOODS and HOBBIES are not, and no amount of good sampling
            fixes a hierarchy built over the wrong set.

      \item \textbf{Complete pooling} denies groups differ; \textbf{no pooling} lets a group with
            8 observations report noise as a finding. You have used both without naming them.

      \item \textbf{Partial pooling} gives each group its own parameter drawn from a shared
            distribution, and estimates how much groups genuinely differ.

      \item \textbf{Shrinkage is automatic and proportionate.} Our thin series moved
            \textbf{8.1$\times$} further toward the group mean than data-rich ones, with nobody
            specifying that --- and on held-out weeks it was \textbf{2.6$\times$} more accurate.

      \item This is \textbf{regularization for the sixth time} --- and the only version where the
            shrinkage strength is estimated rather than tuned.

      \item \textbf{Write it non-centred.} The obvious form gave 127 divergences, the
            reparameterized form 1 --- same model, different geometry.
    \end{enumerate}
    }
  \end{keybox}
  \muted{\itshape Next: Bayesian linear regression --- coefficients as distributions, and what
  you can ask them.}
\end{frame}
